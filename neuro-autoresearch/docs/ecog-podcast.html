01 — The recording

Reading the brain from the inside

Most human neuroscience peers at the brain through the skull — and the skull blurs everything. Intracranial EEG (iEEG), of which electrocorticography (ECoG) is the main flavor, does something rare: it places electrodes directly on or in the living cortex. The signal arrives sharp, fast, and local.

You cannot ethically open a healthy person's skull for science. So intracranial human recordings ride along with medicine. People with drug-resistant epilepsy are sometimes implanted with electrodes for one to two weeks so neurologists can watch their seizures, find exactly where each one starts, and plan surgery. While those electrodes are in place — and with informed consent — patients can volunteer for research.

That clinical-plus-research bargain is the only routine way to obtain millisecond, millimeter recordings from inside the human brain. The trade-offs are real: every patient gets a unique electrode layout dictated by their clinical need, coverage is partial, and cohorts are small. What you gain is a signal no scalp recording can match.

  • Subdural grids & strips — flat sheets of contacts laid on the cortical surface, beneath the dura.
  • Depth / sEEG shafts — thin needles passed into the brain, sampling contacts along their length, including deep folds.
  • Clinical hardware — in this dataset, Ad-Tech electrodes recorded on a Natus NicoletOne system at NYU's Comprehensive Epilepsy Center.

Key terms

ECoG
Electrocorticography — voltage recorded by electrodes sitting on the cortical surface.
iEEG
Intracranial EEG, the umbrella term covering surface ECoG and depth/sEEG.
sEEG
Stereo-EEG: depth electrodes inserted into tissue, sampling along the shaft.
Subdural
Beneath the dura mater (the brain's tough outer membrane), against the cortex.
Channel
One electrode contact's time series — the unit we predict.
02 — The signal

Why ECoG is a once-in-a-method signal

An ECoG channel is a voltage over time — the summed electrical chatter of the neurons just beneath the contact. Decompose it into frequency bands and one band carries most of the cognitive story: the high-gamma range, roughly 70–150 Hz.

High-gamma: a window onto local spiking

Slow rhythms (delta, theta, alpha, beta) reflect large-scale coordination. High-gamma power is different: it tracks, moment to moment, how hard the local population of neurons right under the electrode is firing. It rises within tens of milliseconds when a patch of cortex engages, which makes it the workhorse signal of cognitive ECoG — the closest non-invasive-ish proxy we have to population spiking in the awake human brain.

The dataset ships a ready-made high-gamma preprocessed derivative alongside the raw 512 Hz recording, so analyses can start from this activation signal directly.

Sharp in space and time

Methods usually force a choice. fMRI sees millimeters but smears over seconds, because it measures slow blood flow. Scalp EEG/MEG is fast but the skull and distance blur its spatial origin to centimeters. ECoG, sitting on the cortex, gets both: millisecond timing and millimeter locality. The cost is that it only exists where a clinical electrode happens to be.

Rule of thumb ECoG is the rare method strong on both axes of the trade-off below — paid for by sparse, patient-specific coverage.
03 — The experiment

The Podcast dataset, by the numbers

A simple, powerful paradigm: people listened to one continuous story, doing nothing but paying attention. The transcript — aligned to the audio word by word — opens "Act one, monkey in the middle…" and runs for over five thousand words.

Acquisition clinical rig

Recorded at the NYU Grossman School of Medicine Comprehensive Epilepsy Center. Signals were captured on a Natus NicoletOne system from Ad-Tech intracranial electrodes — a mix of surface grids, strips, and depth shafts placed for each patient's clinical evaluation. Channel metadata (channels.tsv) records electrode type and marks bad contacts.

One story, many brains passive listening

All patients heard the same ~30-minute narrative. Because the recording is the playback, each word's onset in the transcript indexes straight into the iEEG — no extra alignment offset. The shared timeline is what lets researchers ask: does the same word, in the same context, drive comparable neural responses across people and across electrodes?

Why single-subject in our loop The release spans nine subjects (sub-01 … sub-09), but each patient has a different electrode array — there is no common set of channels to pool across. Concatenating them would be meaningless, so analyses (and our harness) work one subject at a time; we default to sub-01.
04 — The question

How the brain turns sound into meaning

Listening to a story is one of the most natural things a brain does — and one of the hardest to study, because it unfolds continuously, in context. A word's neural response depends on every word before it. ECoG's speed and locality make it possible to follow that cascade in real time.

Brains and language models, side by side

This dataset comes out of the Hasson & Goldstein research program at Princeton and NYU, which asks a striking question: do the brain and modern language models represent words in compatible ways? Their answer, across several papers, is broadly yes. The geometry of contextual word embeddings from large language models (GPT-family) lines up with the geometry of neural responses to the same words — a shared model-based linguistic space.

They also found evidence that the brain spontaneously predicts the next word before it arrives, then registers surprise when it is wrong — the same predict-then-update loop that powers a language model, observed in human cortex during ordinary listening.

05 — In neuro-autoresearch

Turning the dataset into an encoding-model benchmark

Our harness treats this recording as a target for an encoding model: push a stimulus through a fixed, never-trained model to get activations, then fit a ridge regression that reads those activations out into recorded brain activity. The held-out test_corr — the mean per-electrode Pearson correlation — is the only reward. The model is never trained; the only thing ever fit is that ridge readout, and that fit is the evaluation.

Stimulus & target

  • X (stimulus): the Mel spectrogram of the podcast audio in each word's [0, 0.5] s window, time-averaged into a per-word Mel-band vector. ECoG is near-instant, so stimulus and neural windows coincide — no HRF delay to decouple (unlike the fMRI presets).
  • Y (target): the subject's ECoG channels over that epoch, resampled to 100 Hz across the 0.5 s window. ECG/MISC channels are dropped, bad contacts are marked from channels.tsv, and constant columns are removed.
  • Subject: a single subject (sub-01, on the order of ~110 ECoG channels) — because each patient's array is unique.

Split & reward

  • Split: a contiguous temporal hold-out — the latest 20% of words by onset are the test set. No random split, because time-adjacent words share signal that would leak.
  • Reward: held-out test_corr = mean per-electrode Pearson r. train_corr is logged too — high train with low test means the readout is overfitting, not progress.
  • Noise: small test_corr differences (under ~0.005) are within noise; only durable gains count.

Run it

Select the dataset with an environment variable and evaluate the current model.py once:

NEURO_DATASET=ieeg python -m harness.run

Structurally this preset is the twin of the meg preset — audio stimulus, per-Word triggers, a fast electrophysiology target with coinciding windows — but the neural signal now comes from electrodes resting on human cortex. Each run appends one row to the per-study CSV; the autoresearch loop proposes a new never-trained model, re-evaluates, and tries to push test_corr higher.
