The Visual Brain — hierarchy, invariance, and the art of seeing.

A grid of retinal light becomes the certain knowledge "that is a face." The ventral stream is the cortical machine that performs this transformation — and the reason a linear readout can ever work.

01 — The wiring

The visual pathway: two streams from one eye

Vision is not a photograph delivered to a screen in your head. It is a cascade of transformations along a chain of cortical areas, each one re-describing the image in a more abstract, more useful vocabulary.

Light hits the retina, which already does real computation — center-surround cells emphasize contrast and discard absolute brightness. The optic nerve carries this to the LGN (lateral geniculate nucleus, a thalamic relay), and from there to V1 (primary visual cortex) at the very back of the brain. From V1 the signal splits into two great highways, a distinction made famous by Ungerleider & Mishkin (1982) and refined by Goodale & Milner (1992):

  • The ventral "what" stream — V1 → V2 → V4 → IT (inferotemporal cortex), flowing forward along the temporal lobe. This is the object-recognition pathway: it answers what am I looking at?
  • The dorsal "where / how" stream — V1 → V2 → up into parietal cortex. It answers where is it, and how do I act on it? — spatial location, motion, and the visual guidance of reaching and grasping (the "how" emphasis is Goodale & Milner's).

For neural encoding of object vision, the protagonist is the ventral stream. Every concept in this chapter lives on it.

In the brain

The two streams are not water-tight: they share early areas, talk to each other, and the "what/where" labels are a useful caricature rather than a hard wall. Goodale & Milner's reframing — "perception vs. action" — came from patient D.F., who could not consciously report an object's orientation yet posted a card through a matching slot flawlessly: seeing for knowing and seeing for doing dissociate.

02 — Before cortex

Retina & LGN: where the hierarchy actually starts

The "V1 → IT" story is so dominant that it is easy to forget the first two stages. But the retina and the LGN already do non-trivial computation — they choose what kind of signal cortex even gets to see.

The output cells of the retina — retinal ganglion cells — and their thalamic targets in the LGN are not simple light meters. Each has a center-surround receptive field: a small central disc and an antagonistic annular surround. An ON-center cell is excited by light in the center and inhibited by light in the surround; an OFF-center cell does the reverse. The practical consequence is that these cells respond to local contrast, not absolute luminance — uniform illumination falls on center and surround equally and roughly cancels. This is the first act of the visual system: throw away the mean, keep the spatial derivative. Computationally it is close to a difference-of-Gaussians (DoG) or Laplacian-of-Gaussian filter, an idea that traces back to Enroth-Cugell & Robson (1966) and the X/Y cell distinction.

The pathway also splits into parallel channels before it ever reaches cortex. The LGN is layered, and two of those layers matter most here:

  • Magnocellular ("magno", M) — large cells, fast, transient, high temporal resolution, color-blind, low contrast threshold. Tuned for motion and coarse, rapid structure; feeds heavily into the dorsal stream.
  • Parvocellular ("parvo", P) — small cells, slower, sustained, high spatial resolution, red–green color-opponent. Tuned for fine detail and color; feeds heavily into the ventral "what" stream.
  • Koniocellular (K) — interlaminar cells carrying a blue–yellow color signal, the often-forgotten third channel.

So even before a single oriented edge is computed, the system has already (a) discarded absolute brightness in favor of contrast, (b) split into ON and OFF streams, and (c) factored the input into fast-coarse-achromatic vs. slow-fine-chromatic channels. V1's oriented simple cells are then built by summing aligned LGN center-surround inputs — Hubel & Wiesel's original feed-forward proposal for where orientation tuning comes from.

Going deeper · feed-forward orientation

Hubel & Wiesel's classic proposal for where orientation tuning comes from: line up several ON-center LGN cells whose centers fall along a row, and a V1 simple cell that sums them will fire best for a bar at exactly that orientation. It is a beautiful, testable wiring hypothesis — and while we now know recurrent and inhibitory circuits sharpen tuning beyond this pure feed-forward sum (the debate runs from Ferster & Miller to the present), the feed-forward skeleton is why a bank of oriented filters is such a good first model of V1.

Quick check — why does an ON-center cell barely respond to uniform light filling its whole receptive field?
Because center and surround are antagonistic. Uniform light excites the ON center but equally drives the inhibitory surround, and the two roughly cancel. The cell is built to report contrast (a spatial difference), so a flat field — which has no contrast — leaves it near baseline. This is exactly the difference-of-Gaussians behavior.

03 — Maps & windows

Retinotopy and the growing receptive field

Early visual cortex is a literal map of the visual field. As you climb the stream, that map blurs — each neuron watches a larger and larger window of the world.

A neuron's receptive field (RF) is the patch of the visual world that can drive it — the region it "watches." In V1, RFs are tiny: a V1 cell near the fovea responds to a spot perhaps a fraction of a degree across. Crucially, V1 is retinotopic — neighboring patches of retina map to neighboring patches of cortex, so V1 holds an orderly, distorted picture of the visual field.

That distortion is cortical magnification: the central fovea — where acuity is highest — claims a hugely disproportionate share of V1 surface, while the periphery is compressed. A thumbnail at the center of gaze recruits far more cortex than the same thumbnail at the edge of vision.

Climb the hierarchy and RFs balloon. By IT, a single neuron's RF can span much of the visual field and often includes the fovea regardless of where the object sits. This RF growth is exactly what makes position tolerance possible: a cell that watches a large window can fire for "face" whether the face is left, right, or centered.

04 — The ladder

The feature hierarchy: edges to objects

Two things increase together as you climb the ventral stream: the complexity of the preferred feature, and the invariance of the response to nuisance changes. This is the central organizing idea of object vision.

V1 — oriented edges. Hubel & Wiesel (Nobel 1981) discovered that V1 "simple cells" act as local, oriented edge-and-bar detectors at a specific position, orientation, and spatial frequency — strikingly well described by Gabor filters (an oriented sinusoid under a Gaussian window). "Complex cells" pool over simple cells to gain a first taste of position tolerance.

V2 / V4 — contours, curvature, texture, color. The next stations combine edges into angles, junctions, and curves (V2 is sensitive to illusory contours and texture statistics; V4 is tuned to curvature, shape parts, hue, and surface texture). Parts of objects begin to take form.

IT — object parts & whole objects. Inferotemporal neurons respond to complex configurations — object parts, and at the top, whole objects and faces — and do so with substantial tolerance to position, size, and pose. Increasing complexity, increasing invariance.

A closer look at V1: orientation tuning

Because V1 is so well characterized, it is the natural place to make "tuning" concrete. A V1 simple cell fires hardest for a bar at its preferred orientation and falls off smoothly as the bar rotates away — a bell-shaped tuning curve. A bank of such cells, tiling orientations and spatial frequencies, is essentially a Gabor wavelet transform of the image — which is why a fixed bank of Gabor/edge filters is a surprisingly strong, learning-free model of early cortex.

Simple vs. complex cells, and the energy model

Hubel & Wiesel did not find one kind of V1 cell — they found two. A simple cell has a receptive field with distinct, side-by-side ON and OFF subregions; it behaves like a linear oriented filter, so it cares about the exact phase (position) of an edge — slide the bar half a cycle and an excitatory response flips to suppression. A complex cell is just as orientation-tuned but phase-invariant: it fires for its preferred orientation anywhere in its little window, whether the edge is light-on-dark or dark-on-light. Complex cells are the first place in the system where a feature ("there is a vertical edge here") survives a small positional nuisance.

How is that invariance built? The standard answer is the energy model of Adelson & Bergen (1985). Take two simple-cell subunits with the same orientation and frequency but receptive fields in quadrature — 90° out of phase, like a sine and a cosine. Square each subunit's output and add them. Because sin²θ + cos²θ = 1, the squared sum no longer depends on where the edge sits within the cycle: you have computed the local Fourier energy at that orientation and frequency. That is exactly a complex cell's behavior, and the very same construction — oriented filters separated in space-time phase — yields a direction-selective motion detector, which is what Adelson & Bergen were really after.

This squaring-and-pooling motif — linear filter → nonlinearity → pool over a nuisance dimension — is the seed of every "complex" stage that follows, all the way up to the convolution-plus-pooling blocks of modern deep nets. Phase invariance in V1, position invariance in IT: same idea, different scale.

Quick check — what is the single most important difference between a simple and a complex cell?
Phase invariance. Both are tuned to orientation (and spatial frequency), but a simple cell has segregated ON/OFF subregions and so is sensitive to the exact position/polarity (phase) of an edge, whereas a complex cell responds to its preferred orientation regardless of phase or exact location within its RF. The energy model builds that invariance by squaring and summing two quadrature simple-cell subunits.

05 — The core idea

Invariance & "untangling": why a line can read identity

This is the conceptual heart of the chapter, and the deep link back to Chapter 01's linear-readout idea. The ventral stream's job is to make object identity linearly separable.

Think of every image as a single point in a very high-dimensional space — one axis per pixel. Now take all the images of a particular cat, photographed under every pose, size, lighting, and position, and trace out the set of points they occupy. That set is a curved surface — an object manifold. The manifold for a cat and the manifold for a dog are each smooth, but in pixel space they are hopelessly tangled, folded through one another like two sheets of crumpled paper. No straight cut (no linear classifier) can separate them.

DiCarlo & Cox (2007) framed object recognition as exactly this problem of untangling, and DiCarlo, Zoccolan & Rust (2012) made it the organizing principle of the ventral stream. Each stage applies a transformation that gently flattens and straightens these manifolds. By the time the representation reaches IT, the cat and dog manifolds have been pulled apart into compact, near-flat blobs — and now a simple linear readout (a hyperplane, a weighted sum) can cleanly separate them. Identity has become explicit.

Key idea

Object recognition is not about finding a clever boundary in pixel space — none exists. It is about re-formatting the representation until a trivial boundary works. The ventral stream is a sequence of transformations engineered (by evolution and development) to make identity linearly decodable. A linear encoding model that predicts IT well is, in effect, measuring how far that untangling has gone.

06 — Mid & high vision

Beyond V1: textures in V2, curvature in V4, faces in IT

The middle of the ventral stream is where the story gets specific. V2, V4 and IT each have a signature computation that has been pinned down with real experiments — and IT hides one of the most elegant codes in all of neuroscience.

V2 — higher-order texture statistics. What does V2 compute that V1 cannot? Freeman, Ziemba, Movshon, Simoncelli & Heeger (2013) gave a sharp answer. They built two kinds of stimuli: "noise" textures matched to an image only in its V1-level (spectral) statistics, and "naturalistic" textures that additionally matched the higher-order correlations across orientation and scale that the Portilla–Simoncelli model captures. V1 neurons could not tell them apart; V2 neurons responded more to the naturalistic textures, and human fMRI showed the same V2-but-not-V1 preference. V2's job, then, is the statistics of how edges co-occur — the texture of surfaces.

V4 — curvature and shape parts. Pasupathy & Connor (2001, 2002) showed that V4 neurons are tuned to boundary curvature at a particular angular position relative to the object's center — e.g., "a sharp convexity pointing to the upper right." A handful of such curvature-and-position parts can compose a remarkable range of closed shapes. V4 is where a contour stops being a generic edge and becomes a described shape fragment; it is also strongly color- and surface-tuned.

IT — columns, face patches, and an axis code. Inferotemporal cortex is organized into columns of neurons that share a complex object-feature preference. Within it sits the macaque face-patch system: a network of discrete, fMRI-localizable patches (ML/MF, AL, AM…) that Tsao & Freiwald (2006) showed are almost entirely face-selective and are wired together as a processing hierarchy — posterior patches tolerate less view change, anterior patch AM codes face identity nearly invariant to viewpoint. The human FFA is widely read as the homolog of this system.

And how does a face patch encode a face? Not as a lookup table of templates. Chang & Tsao (2017) found that face-patch neurons implement an axis code: each cell computes a linear projection of the face onto one axis of a ~50-dimensional face space (shape + appearance features). A given cell is tuned to a direction, not a specific face, and is flat (unselective) along the orthogonal directions. The stunning payoff: from the responses of only ~200 cells they could reconstruct the actual face the monkey was viewing — and predict a cell's response to any face from its projection onto that cell's axis. This is a linear, geometric code, and it is precisely the kind of representation a ridge readout is built to exploit.

Going deeper · norm-based face space

The axis code is the neural realization of an old psychological idea — Valentine's (1991) norm-based face space, where faces are encoded as deviations from an average face. It also dovetails with untangling: if identity lives along explicit linear axes, then identity is, by construction, linearly decodable — the manifold has been straightened into a coordinate system. Note the contrast with V1's bell-shaped tuning: face cells are ramp-tuned (monotonic along their axis), which is exactly what makes a weighted-sum readout so effective.

07 — A canonical computation

Divisive normalization: the cortex's repeated motif

One operation shows up again and again across areas, modalities, and species — so often that Carandini & Heeger (2012) called it a canonical neural computation: divisive normalization.

The idea is simple. A neuron's driven response is not reported on its own; it is divided by the pooled activity of a surrounding population of neurons (the "normalization pool"). Formally, the response is roughly R = D / (σ + Σ pool) — a numerator that is the cell's own drive, over a denominator that grows with how active its neighbors are. Turn up the whole population and every individual response is scaled down.

This one operation explains a startling range of phenomena with the same equation: V1 contrast saturation and cross-orientation suppression (a mask at a different orientation suppresses the response even though it drives the cell little on its own), surround suppression, light adaptation in the retina, the effects of attention, and the winner-take-all sharpening of population codes. Functionally it does several useful things at once: it keeps responses within a usable dynamic range (gain control), it decorrelates / whitens the population (improving efficiency, à la Barlow), and it makes responses more invariant to overall contrast. Modern deep nets rediscovered close cousins of it — local response normalization, batch/layer norm, and the squaring-and-pooling of complex cells is itself a normalization-adjacent motif.

Quick check — cross-orientation suppression: a mask at the orthogonal orientation barely drives a V1 cell on its own, yet adding it suppresses the cell's response to its preferred grating. How does normalization explain this?
The orthogonal mask contributes little to the numerator (the cell's own drive), but it does activate other cells in the normalization pool, so it inflates the denominator. A bigger denominator divides the response down. Suppression without much direct drive is the signature prediction of divisive normalization — and a reason a purely linear filter model of V1 systematically over-predicts at high contrast.

08 — Neighborhoods

Category-selective cortex — and the distributed counter-view

High-level ventral cortex is not a uniform sheet. Certain patches respond far more strongly to one category than to others — yet object identity is also carried by activity spread across the whole region. Both views are true.

Functional MRI revealed several reliable, category-selective patches on the ventral surface of the temporal and occipital lobes:

  • FFA — fusiform face area; responds preferentially to faces (Kanwisher, McDermott & Chun, 1997).
  • PPA — parahippocampal place area; scenes & places, spatial layout (Epstein & Kanwisher, 1998).
  • EBA — extrastriate body area; bodies & body parts (Downing et al., 2001).
  • VWFA — visual word form area; letter strings & words (left occipitotemporal; Cohen & Dehaene).
  • LOC — lateral occipital complex; objects/shape in general, the more domain-general object responder.

But selectivity is not the whole story. Haxby et al. (2001) showed that the identity of a viewed category can be decoded from the distributed pattern of activity across ventral temporal cortex — including the voxels outside a category's "preferred" region, and even after excluding the peak region entirely. So the same cortex supports two readings: modular (dedicated patches for special categories) and distributed (a population code where every object is a pattern across the whole map). Present both fairly: the patches are real and robust, and the distributed code is real and robust.

Watch out

"FFA = the face module" is a useful shorthand, not a literal claim that faces live in one spot and nowhere else. FFA responds most to faces, but it carries graded information about many categories, and face information is also present elsewhere. Modular and distributed coding are two lenses on the same tissue — don't read either as the whole truth.

Going deeper · modular vs distributed, and the reverse hierarchy

The honest summary of the Kanwisher (modular: dedicated, possibly innate patches for special, evolutionarily important categories) vs. Haxby (distributed: object identity is a graded pattern across all of ventral temporal cortex) debate is that both effects are real and reproducible, and the modern view holds them together — strong category peaks superimposed on a continuous, topographically smooth feature map (the "what" map of Grill-Spector & Weiner). The disagreement is mostly about emphasis and what counts as the unit of analysis, not about the data.

A third nuance complicates the strict "feed-forward only" reading of the hierarchy: the reverse-hierarchy theory of Hochstein & Ahissar (2002). Conscious perception, they argue, starts at the top — fast, gist-level "vision at a glance" read out from high areas with large RFs — and only descends to early areas (with their fine spatial detail) when the task demands "vision with scrutiny." Recurrent and feedback connections, not just the bottom-up sweep, shape what you actually see. A purely feed-forward encoding model is a first approximation, not the last word.

Quick check — Haxby showed category identity is decodable even after removing a category's peak region. Why does that not refute the existence of the FFA?
Because "there is extra, distributed information outside region X" and "region X is strongly and reliably selective" are both true — they are not contradictory. Removing the FFA still leaves a usable distributed pattern, but that does not make the FFA's face preference disappear or unreliable. The lesson is that selectivity (a peak) and a population code (spread information) coexist in the same cortex; the debate is about emphasis, not about whether either effect is real.

09 — The modeling gradient

Why early areas are "easy" and category regions are "hard"

Everything above predicts a clean experimental signature — one this project measures directly.

Early visual areas (V1 V2 V3) compute relatively low-level, generic features: oriented edges, local contrast, spatial frequency, simple color and texture statistics. These are exactly the features you can write down by hand — a Gabor bank, a few color-opponent channels, some local-energy pooling. So a fixed, untrained model built from such primitives can explain early cortex well, because early cortex is doing something close to those primitives.

High-level category regions (FFA PPA EBA VWFA) compute learned, abstract, category-tuned features — the product of the full untangling cascade. You cannot guess the right basis for "face-ness" or "place-ness" by hand; it has to be learned from experience with objects. That is precisely where untrained models stumble and a network trained on object recognition pulls ahead — making the high-level ventral stream a clean probe of "architecture vs. learning."

In this project

Test yourself

Check your understanding

Pick an answer to see why it's right or wrong. Score: 0 / 8

  1. 1. A patient can correctly post a card through a tilted slot but cannot verbally report the slot's orientation. This dissociation is the clearest evidence for which distinction?

    Why: This is patient D.F. and Goodale & Milner's reframing of the two streams as perception (ventral, conscious "what") vs. action (dorsal, "how"). She lacks conscious form perception yet her dorsal action system computes the orientation needed to guide the hand. The other options are real distinctions but not what this behavioral dissociation demonstrates.

  2. 2. As you ascend retina → V1 → V4 → IT, what is the most accurate description of how receptive fields change?

    Why: RFs grow dramatically — from a fraction of a degree in V1 to tens of degrees in IT. That pooling over a large window is precisely the substrate of position tolerance: an IT cell can signal "face" wherever the face lands. Retinotopy correspondingly coarsens, not sharpens.

  3. 3. The energy model (Adelson & Bergen) builds a phase-invariant complex cell by…

    Why: Two same-orientation filters in quadrature (sine and cosine phase), each squared and added, give a response independent of the edge's exact phase (because sin²+cos² is constant) while preserving orientation tuning. That is the local energy / complex-cell computation. Averaging across orientations would destroy orientation tuning; thresholding one linear filter stays phase-sensitive.

  4. 4. "Untangling" the ventral stream means, most precisely, that…

    Why: In pixel space the manifolds for two objects are smooth but hopelessly interleaved — no hyperplane separates them. The stream applies transformations that flatten and pull them apart so that in IT a linear readout suffices. It is not about finding a curved boundary (none works in pixel space) and not about discarding all nuisance info — IT still carries position, just in a format where identity is explicit.

  5. 5. Chang & Tsao's "axis code" for faces implies that a single face-patch neuron…

    Why: Each cell computes a linear projection onto its preferred axis and ramps with position along that axis while ignoring orthogonal changes. It is tuned to a direction in face space, not a particular face — the opposite of a grandmother cell. Because the code is linear, ~200 cells suffice to reconstruct the viewed face.

  6. 6. The PPA (parahippocampal place area) responds most strongly to which of the following?

    Why: Epstein & Kanwisher (1998) showed the PPA prefers scenes and the geometry of local space. Faces → FFA, words → VWFA (left-lateralized), bodies → EBA. Keeping these four mappings straight is core to reading the ventral category map.

  7. 7. A V1 cell is driven by a vertical grating. Adding a superimposed horizontal mask — which barely drives the cell on its own — suppresses its response. Divisive normalization explains this because the mask…

    Why: Cross-orientation suppression is the signature of normalization: the orthogonal mask adds little to the numerator (own drive) but activates other cells in the pool, enlarging the denominator in R = D/(σ + Σ pool), so the response divides down. It is not classical (within-RF) inhibition and it does not change the cell's preferred orientation.

  8. 8. Why do fixed, untrained hand-designed models predict V1–V3 well but fall short in FFA/PPA?

    Why: Gabors, color-opponent channels and local energy pooling are roughly what early cortex computes, so a hand-written model matches it. "Face-ness" or "place-ness" is the product of the full untangling cascade and cannot be guessed by hand — it has to be learned, which is exactly where a network trained on object recognition pulls ahead. That contrast is the "architecture vs. learning" probe this project runs.

11 — Glossary

Key terms

Ventral stream
The "what" pathway V1 → V2 → V4 → IT; builds invariant object identity.
Dorsal stream
The "where / how" pathway toward parietal cortex; spatial location and visually guided action.
Retinotopy
The orderly map of the visual field onto cortex; the fovea is magnified (over-represented).
Receptive field (RF)
The region of the visual world that can drive a neuron; grows from tiny (V1) to large (IT).
V1 / V4 / IT
Early (edges), mid (curves·color·texture), and high (objects·faces·identity) stages of the stream.
Invariance
A response that holds across nuisance changes — position, size, pose, lighting.
Untangling
Re-formatting tangled object manifolds so identity becomes linearly separable in IT (DiCarlo & Cox).
FFA / PPA / EBA / VWFA
Category-selective patches for faces / places / bodies / visual word forms.
Category selectivity
A region responding far more to one category than others — coexisting with a distributed population code.
Cortical magnification
The disproportionate cortical area devoted to the high-acuity foveal center of gaze.
Center-surround / DoG
Retinal/LGN RFs with an antagonistic center and surround; report local contrast, not brightness (difference-of-Gaussians).
Magno / parvo
Parallel LGN channels: fast achromatic magno (→ dorsal) vs. fine color-opponent parvo (→ ventral).
Simple / complex cell
V1 cells: simple = phase-sensitive oriented filter; complex = phase-invariant via energy pooling.
Energy model
Square two quadrature simple-cell subunits and sum → phase-invariant complex cell (Adelson & Bergen 1985).
Axis code
Face-patch neurons report a face's linear projection onto a tuning axis of face space (Chang & Tsao 2017).
Face-patch system
Networked, near-purely face-selective patches in macaque IT (Tsao & Freiwald); FFA is the likely human homolog.
Divisive normalization
A canonical computation: response = own drive ÷ pooled neighbor activity; explains saturation, suppression, gain control.
Reverse hierarchy
Conscious perception begins at high areas (gist) and descends to early areas for detail (Hochstein & Ahissar 2002).

Findings synthesized from established systems-neuroscience literature (Hubel & Wiesel; Ungerleider & Mishkin 1982; Goodale & Milner 1992; Kanwisher 1997; Epstein & Kanwisher 1998; Downing 2001; Haxby 2001; DiCarlo & Cox 2007; DiCarlo, Zoccolan & Rust 2012). No results invented.
