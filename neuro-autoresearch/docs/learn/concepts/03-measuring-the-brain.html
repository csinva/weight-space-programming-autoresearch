Measuring the Brain

From spikes to BOLD — every instrument that listens to neural activity is an indirect, partial window. Some are sharp in space, some sharp in time, some non-invasive, none see everything. This chapter is a tour of those windows, and of how each one is turned into the target a model is asked to predict.

You'll understand

  • What a spike, normalized MUA, and the ECoG high-gamma band actually measure.
  • What physically generates EEG/MEG, and why MEG mostly sees tangential (sulcal) sources.
  • Why EEG/MEG source localization is an ill-posed inverse problem.
  • Canonical brain rhythms and the aperiodic 1/f background they ride on.
  • What BOLD does and doesn't measure — T2*, the HRF lag, and the BOLD nonlinearity.
  • How sampling, filtering, re-referencing, and trial averaging (√N) turn a messy trace into one number per voxel/electrode per stimulus.

01 — The measurement problem

No instrument sees the whole brain

The brain is ~86 billion neurons firing on a millisecond clock, packed at micrometer scale, threaded through three pounds of tissue behind a bone shield. No device built — or buildable — captures all of that at once. Every method is a transduction: it converts some physical correlate of neural activity (a voltage, a magnetic field, a change in blood oxygen) into a number, and in doing so it throws most of the activity away.

Three axes govern the choice. Spatial resolution — how finely can you localize where the signal came from? Temporal resolution — how finely can you resolve when? Invasiveness & coverage — does it require surgery, and does it see one spot or the whole brain? The brutal truth is that these trade off against each other, and the methods that win on both space and time are exactly the ones you can only use in a clinic or an animal.

Key idea

There is no free lunch. The instrument that is fast and sharp in space (a microelectrode) is invasive and sees almost nothing of the brain. The instrument that sees the whole brain non-invasively (fMRI) is slow and indirect. Picking a method is picking which truths you can afford to lose.

Throughout this chapter, keep one downstream fact in mind: in an encoding model (Chapter 1), the measured signal is the target Y — the thing a model's activations are regressed onto. So a method's quirks become the model's problem. The lag of the blood signal, the blur of the skull, the sparseness of an electrode array — all of it shapes what "predicting the brain" even means.

02 — Microelectrodes

Single units, multi-units, and the spike

Go all the way in. Push a metal microelectrode — tip on the order of a micrometer — into the cortex, and you sit close enough to individual cells to overhear the brain's actual currency: the action potential, or spike. A spike is a ~1 ms all-or-none voltage event a neuron emits to communicate. This is the most direct, highest-spatial-precision window we have.

The electrode picks up the extracellular potential — the voltage in the fluid around cells. When a nearby neuron spikes, it leaves a brief, stereotyped deflection. If one cell dominates the recording, you can spike-sort and isolate a single unit (one putative neuron). When several nearby cells contribute and can't be teased apart, you have multi-unit activity (MUA) — the blended spiking of a small local population.

For encoding models, the convenient readout is normalized MUA: band-pass the trace to the spiking band, take its envelope (rectify + smooth) over a window, and average — collapsing a burst of spikes into one number per electrode per stimulus that says "how hard did this patch fire?" That scalar is exactly the kind of target an encoding model wants.

03 — Intracranial

ECoG, iEEG, and the high-gamma band

Back off from inside single cells to the cortical surface and you reach electrocorticography (ECoG) — part of the broader family iEEG (intracranial EEG). Subdural grids and strips lie on the cortex; depth (sEEG) shafts go into it. You lose single-cell resolution but gain coverage of a region — while keeping millisecond timing and millimeter locality.

An ECoG channel is a voltage over time. Decompose it into frequency bands and one band carries most of the cognitive signal: the high-gamma band, ~70–150 Hz. High-gamma power tracks, moment to moment, how hard the local population of neurons under the electrode is firing — it is the best non-microelectrode proxy for population spiking in the awake human brain, and it rises within tens of milliseconds when a patch of cortex engages.

The catch is access: human ECoG exists only because people with drug-resistant epilepsy are implanted for clinical seizure mapping and consent to research while the electrodes are in. Coverage is dictated by medical need, every patient's array is different, and cohorts are small. Within those constraints, ECoG is the rare method strong on both resolution axes.

In the brain

Why high-gamma? It isn't a true rhythm but a broadband power increase that co-varies with the local spike rate. So when you can't reach single cells, high-gamma power is the closest you get to "this patch is busy right now" — which is why cognitive ECoG lives in this band.

04 — Outside the skull

EEG, MEG, and the inverse problem

Now come all the way out, to the scalp. EEG reads tiny electrical potentials at the head surface; MEG reads the minute magnetic fields those same currents produce. Both are non-invasive and, crucially, millisecond-fast — they sample the electromagnetic consequence of neural activity directly, with no sluggish intermediary. The price is paid in space.

What generates the signal? Not spikes — they're too fast and too local to summate at the scalp. The measurable field comes from synchronized post-synaptic currents in the apical dendrites of pyramidal cells, which are aligned perpendicular to the cortical sheet. Thousands of these neighboring dendrites, currents flowing the same way at the same time, act like a tiny current dipole. EEG/MEG sees the summed dipoles of large, synchronously-active cortical patches.

And here is the deep problem. Given a pattern of voltages/fields across the sensors, where in the brain did it come from? This is the inverse problem, and it is mathematically ill-posed: by a classic result (Helmholtz), infinitely many internal source configurations produce the exact same external field. The forward direction (sources → sensors) is unique; the inverse (sensors → sources) is not. You cannot recover the sources from the data alone — you must add assumptions (regularized priors: minimum-norm, sparsity, anatomical constraints) to pick one solution. Compounding it, volume conduction / field spread smears each source across many sensors.

Open vs closed fields — why pyramidal cells dominate

Not every active neuron contributes. A cell only produces a far field if its current sources and sinks are spatially separated along an axis. Pyramidal neurons have a long apical dendrite running radially through the cortex, so an excitatory synapse on the dendrite (current sink) and the return current at the soma (source) form an open field — a genuine dipole whose field reaches the scalp. Stellate interneurons with radially symmetric dendrites form a closed field: their currents cancel by symmetry and are nearly invisible from outside. EEG/MEG is therefore biased toward layered, columnar pyramidal populations whose dipoles point the same way and fire in sync (Buzsáki, Anastassiou & Koch, 2012).

Why MEG mostly sees the sulci

EEG and MEG read the same dipoles but with different geometric sensitivity. A current dipole has an orientation: on a gyral crown the cortex faces outward, so the dipole is radial (pointing at the skull); in a sulcal wall the cortex is folded vertical, so the dipole is tangential (parallel to the skull). By the Biot–Savart law a purely radial dipole in a spherically symmetric head produces no external magnetic field — its field lines close inside the head. So MEG is almost blind to radial (gyral) sources and chiefly sees tangential (sulcal) ones, while EEG, which senses volume-conducted potentials, picks up both radial and tangential generators (Hämäläinen et al., 1993; Hillebrand & Barnes, 2002).

Sensors: magnetometers, gradiometers, and transparency

MEG fields are ~10−13 T — a billionth of the Earth's field — so it is measured with SQUID (or newer optically-pumped) sensors inside a shielded room. A magnetometer reads the raw field and is most sensitive to deep sources but also to distant environmental noise; a gradiometer subtracts two nearby pickups to measure the spatial gradient, cancelling far-away uniform noise and sharpening focus on superficial sources. The decisive contrast with EEG is physical: skull and scalp have wildly varying conductivity that smears and attenuates volume-conducted potentials, whereas the head is magnetically transparent (tissue permeability ≈ that of free space), so MEG fields pass through undistorted — buying MEG cleaner forward models, at the cost of seeing only tangential sources.

Quick check — Why is MEG essentially blind to a dipole sitting on a gyral crown?
Because a gyral-crown dipole is radial (points straight at the skull), and a radial current dipole in a roughly spherical head produces no net magnetic field outside the head — its field lines close inside. EEG still detects it via volume-conducted potentials, but MEG sees mainly tangential (sulcal-wall) sources.
Going deeper

The forward model that maps a unit dipole to each sensor is the lead field. Two facts fall out of it: (1) sensitivity drops steeply with depth (roughly with the inverse square of distance), so both EEG and MEG favor superficial cortex and struggle with deep structures like the hippocampus; (2) because MEG's lead field for radial sources is ~zero, its rank is effectively lower than EEG's — which is one reason combining EEG and MEG constrains the inverse problem better than either alone.

Watch out

"MEG/EEG has great spatial resolution because it's so fast" — no. Speed and space are separate axes. EEG/MEG are superb in time (ms) and weak in space (cm-scale, and ambiguous because of the inverse problem). A sharp temporal signal localized to the wrong place is still mislocalized.

05 — Rhythms & the background

Oscillations and the 1/f spectrum

Whenever a method records a continuous voltage or field — EEG, MEG, LFP, ECoG — the natural next move is to look at its power spectrum: how much energy sits at each frequency. Two things live in that spectrum, and conflating them is one of the field's most common mistakes: narrow oscillatory peaks riding on a broad aperiodic 1/f background.

The peaks are the canonical bands. Delta (~1–4 Hz, deep sleep), theta (~4–8 Hz, memory and navigation), alpha (~8–13 Hz, the famous occipital idle rhythm), beta (~13–30 Hz, motor maintenance), and gamma (~30–80 Hz, local circuit activity). Each is a genuine rhythm: synchronized populations cycling, visible as a bump above the background at a characteristic frequency.

But underneath every spectrum is a smooth 1/fβ slope — power falling off as frequency rises, straight on log–log axes. This aperiodic component is not noise to be ignored: its slope (exponent β) and offset shift with arousal, age, anaesthesia, and the local balance of excitation and inhibition. Voytek and colleagues showed that failing to separate the periodic peaks from this aperiodic background causes spurious "oscillation" effects that are really just slope changes (Donoghue et al., 2020).

Why high-gamma is special

This reframes the high-gamma signal from §04. A real oscillation is a peak at a fixed frequency; high-gamma is not that. Ray and Maunsell showed that the 70–150 Hz "high-gamma" is a broadband power increase — the whole high-frequency tail of the 1/f spectrum lifting together — that tracks the local spike rate, distinct from the narrowband ~40 Hz gamma rhythm (Ray & Maunsell, 2011; cf. Crone et al. on the cognitive uses of high-gamma). That is why the project's ECoG datasets read out high-gamma power as a firing proxy: it is the aperiodic component, not a rhythm, that carries the spike-rate signal.

Quick check — Why is high-gamma power treated as a firing proxy rather than a "gamma oscillation"?
Because it is a broadband elevation — the entire high-frequency tail of the aperiodic 1/f spectrum rising together — that co-varies with the local spike rate, not a narrowband peak at a fixed frequency. The classic ~40 Hz narrowband gamma rhythm is a peak; high-gamma is the slope/offset of the background shifting. Conflating the two (as FOOOF/specparam warns) produces spurious oscillation effects.
Going deeper

The aperiodic exponent has a mechanistic reading: in models of summed synaptic currents, a steeper 1/f slope corresponds to more inhibition relative to excitation (slower decay of the population current), so β is used as a non-invasive E/I-balance index. Practically, tools like specparam (FOOOF) fit the spectrum as aperiodic + Σ peaks so that a change in alpha power isn't accidentally a change in the broadband floor — the same caution that makes high-gamma a firing index rather than a rhythm.

06 — Hemodynamics

fMRI and the BOLD signal

The most widely used window on the human brain measures neither voltage nor magnetism of neurons — it measures blood. fMRI's BOLD signal (Blood-Oxygen-Level-Dependent) exploits neurovascular coupling: when a patch of cortex becomes active, it triggers a local increase in blood flow that overshoots the oxygen demand, transiently raising the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated hemoglobin — which is magnetically detectable.

This buys two great things: millimeter spatial resolution and whole-brain coverage, all non-invasively. The brain is carved into voxels (3D pixels, ~1–3 mm), each reporting its local BOLD over time. But the signal is indirect and slow. Blood flow is not instantaneous: the vascular response is described by the hemodynamic response function (HRF), a delayed, smoothed curve that rises after the neural event, peaks ~4–6 s later, and returns through a post-stimulus undershoot over ~20 s.

So fMRI is a low-pass-filtered, lagged shadow of neural activity. A 50 ms neural burst becomes a several-second blood bump. You cannot resolve fast dynamics — but you can ask, voxel by voxel across the whole brain, "which patches responded to this stimulus?"

The physics: deoxyhemoglobin and T2*

Why is blood oxygenation magnetic at all? Because deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic while oxyhemoglobin is not. Deoxyhemoglobin distorts the local magnetic field around each vessel, and these microscopic field inhomogeneities make spinning protons dephase faster — they shorten the tissue's T2* (the apparent transverse-relaxation time). BOLD imaging is a T2*-weighted sequence (typically gradient-echo EPI): when activity raises blood flow and flushes out deoxyhemoglobin, the field becomes more uniform, T2* lengthens, and the measured signal goes up. So a "bright" BOLD response literally means "less deoxyhemoglobin here than baseline" — a vascular, not a neural, quantity. Logothetis's simultaneous recordings showed BOLD tracks the local field potential (synaptic input and local processing) more tightly than the output spike rate (Logothetis et al., 2001).

The BOLD nonlinearity — and what a voxel pools

BOLD is not a clean linear readout of "amount of activity." For brief or intense stimuli the response saturates: doubling the stimulus duration or contrast does not double the BOLD amplitude, and very short events evoke a larger response than a strictly linear convolution predicts. This compressive nonlinearity comes partly from the vasculature (flow can only increase so much) and partly from neural adaptation, and it means encoding models must reason about a saturating target, not a proportional one. Add to that the physiological noise riding on every voxel — cardiac pulsation, respiration, head motion, scanner drift — much of it structured and stimulus-unrelated. And remember what a voxel actually pools: a 2 mm cube holds on the order of a million neurons plus their glia and a dense vascular bed, all averaged into one number per TR. fMRI never sees a neuron; it sees the blood-borne aftermath of a whole local population.

Quick check — A BOLD voxel gets brighter. Did the local deoxyhemoglobin go up or down?
Down. Deoxyhemoglobin is paramagnetic and shortens T2*; activity drives an over-supply of oxygenated blood that washes out deoxyhemoglobin, lengthening T2* and raising the T2*-weighted signal. Brighter BOLD = less deoxyhemoglobin = (indirectly) more local activity, ~5 s earlier.
Going deeper

The Buxton "balloon model" derives the HRF and its undershoot from physiology: the BOLD signal is set by the interplay of cerebral blood flow (CBF), blood volume (CBV), and the cerebral metabolic rate of oxygen (CMRO₂). Flow rises faster and farther than oxygen consumption, so deoxyhemoglobin drops (the positive lobe); the post-stimulus undershoot is largely a slow return of dilated venous volume after flow has normalized. This is also why BOLD amplitude is not directly comparable across regions or people — the vascular gain differs.

In this project

For NSD (human fMRI, vision) and the Le Petit Prince fMRI, the harness does not read the response at the stimulus onset — it shifts the window forward by offset ≈ 5 s to the HRF peak and averages the peak TRs into one value per voxel (a betas-like estimate). 08 — From timeseries to target

What counts as "a response"

A raw recording is a long, noisy timeseries — not the clean target a model can be scored against. The final step in every modality is the same in spirit: collapse the mess into one number per voxel/electrode per stimulus. Get there with two tools — averaging to fight noise, and a window chosen to catch the real signal at the right latency.

Averaging fights noise

Single trials are dominated by noise — thermal, physiological, instrumental. Because the stimulus-locked response repeats while noise does not, averaging across repeats (or trials) lifts the signal out. In NSD, the special shared-1000 images are seen multiple times precisely so their responses can be averaged into stable targets.

Windowing catches the signal

Each method needs its own window. For fMRI, slide the window forward to the HRF peak (~5 s) and average those TRs — a betas-like estimate. For spiking/ECoG/MEG, the response is near-instant, so the window sits right on the stimulus. Wrong window, wrong (or zero) signal.

In this project

This is exactly how the harness builds Y, per modality. For fMRI (NSD, Le Petit Prince) it shifts the window forward ~5 s to the HRF peak and averages TRs into a betas-like value; for MEG and ECoG the window coincides with the word because the signal is near-instant. Same target shape, different treatment — the method's physics dictates the recipe. Dataset guides: Podcast ECoG · Le Petit Prince · TVSD macaque.

09 — The processing pipeline

From signal to data

Between the electrode/coil and the scalar y sits a pipeline of unglamorous-but-decisive steps. Each is a choice that shapes the target a model must predict; get one wrong and you score against an artefact. Here are the load-bearing ones.

Sampling & the Nyquist limit

A continuous signal is digitized by sampling it at a fixed rate. The Nyquist theorem says you can faithfully represent only frequencies below half the sampling rate — so to keep the 70–150 Hz high-gamma band you need to sample well above 300 Hz, and clinical iEEG often runs at 512–2000 Hz. Anything above Nyquist doesn't vanish: it aliases, masquerading as a spurious low frequency. An anti-alias low-pass filter is therefore applied before the analog-to-digital step, not after.

Filtering

Recorded traces are then band-limited to the range of interest: high-pass to drop slow drift and DC offset, low-pass/notch to suppress line noise (50/60 Hz) and out-of-band junk. Splitting a wideband electrode trace into the spiking band (~300–3000 Hz, for MUA) versus the LFP band (<300 Hz) is just filtering. Beware: aggressive or non-causal filters can smear activity across time and create acausal "pre-onset" responses — a known trap in ERP work.

Re-referencing

Every voltage is measured relative to a reference, so the reference is part of the signal. EEG often uses an average or mastoid reference; intracranial work uses a bipolar montage (each channel = neighbor minus neighbor, which localizes signal between contacts and kills shared noise) or a common-average reference (CAR) (subtract the mean across all good channels). Re-referencing changes what each "channel" even means — a bipolar high-gamma channel reflects the patch between two contacts.

Spike sorting

To go from a microelectrode trace to single units, detected spike waveforms are clustered by shape (PCA on the snippets, then a mixture model) so each cluster is one putative neuron. It is error-prone — overlapping spikes, drift, and unstable clusters — which is exactly why encoding work often prefers the cluster-free MUA envelope from §02.

Epoching & trial averaging — the √N law

Finally, the continuous record is cut into epochs time-locked to each stimulus, and repeats are averaged. The payoff is quantitative: if trial-to-trial noise is independent with standard deviation σ, averaging N trials shrinks the noise to σ/√N while the stimulus-locked signal stays put — so SNR grows as √N. Quadrupling repeats only halves the noise: diminishing returns, which is why scan time and number of repeats are precious. This is precisely why NSD shows its shared-1000 images multiple times.

Quick check — You average 100 trials instead of 25. How much does SNR improve?
2×. SNR grows as √N, so going from 25 to 100 trials is √(100/25) = √4 = 2. (Not 4× — the √ is the whole point, and the reason more repeats give diminishing returns.) This assumes the trial-to-trial noise is independent; correlated noise — drift, motion, slow physiology — averages away more slowly.
Going deeper

The √N benefit assumes independent noise; real recordings have structured, correlated noise (slow drift, heartbeat, motion) that does not shrink as √N. This is why fMRI uses GLM nuisance regressors (motion parameters, physiological traces) and why ceiling estimates use a noise ceiling (the across-repeat reliability sets the maximum r any model could achieve). A model can't be more predictive than the data is reliable — so √N also bounds your headline correlation.

In this project

The harness inherits the clean end of this pipeline: it loads already-preprocessed responses (filtered, referenced, epoched) and does the last two steps — average repeats, window to the right latency — to produce Y. The √N law is why the noise ceiling matters in Chapter 1: test_corr is judged against how reliable the averaged target is, not against a perfect 1.0.

Pick an answer to see why it's right or wrong. Score: 0 / 8

  1. 1. A method gives you sub-millisecond timing and pinpoints single neurons. By the trade-offs in this chapter, what almost certainly had to be sacrificed?

    Why: The fast-and-fine corner is bought only by going invasive and sparse (microelectrodes, ECoG). Space and time can be co-purchased, but only by giving up non-invasive whole-brain coverage — there is no free lunch.

  2. 2. Why is the EEG/MEG inverse problem ill-posed rather than merely hard?

    Why: The forward direction is unique; the inverse is not. By Helmholtz's result, many different source layouts (e.g. one deep strong dipole vs several shallow weak ones) yield the same sensor pattern — so the data alone can't pick one and you must add priors. Better sensors don't fix a fundamental non-uniqueness.

  3. 3. Why is MEG largely insensitive to a current dipole sitting on a gyral crown?

    Why: Gyral-crown dipoles are radial, and a radial dipole in a roughly spherical head generates no net field outside the head (its lines close internally). So MEG mainly sees tangential sulcal-wall sources. The skull is magnetically transparent — it doesn't shield — and the effect is geometric, not about speed or cell type.

  4. 4. ECoG "high-gamma" (70–150 Hz) is best described as…

    Why: High-gamma is a broadband lift of the aperiodic spectral tail that co-varies with the local spike rate (Ray & Maunsell, 2011) — not a true oscillation (that's the separate ~40 Hz narrowband gamma) and not single units. It's the best non-microelectrode firing proxy in awake humans.

  5. 5. A BOLD voxel gets brighter during a task. What did it most directly measure?

    Why: BOLD is T2*-weighted: activity drives a flow overshoot that washes out paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin, lengthening T2* and raising the signal — all peaking ~5 s after the neural event. It's a vascular, hemodynamically-lagged proxy that tracks local field potentials (input/processing) more than spike output (Logothetis et al., 2001), and metabolic O₂ use actually rises less than flow.

  6. 6. A 4-second stimulus produces only ~2× the BOLD amplitude of a 1-second one. This is because…

    Why: The BOLD nonlinearity: amplitude is a compressive function of duration/contrast (vascular ceiling plus neural adaptation), so it sits above the linear prediction for brief events and saturates below it for long ones. So the target a model predicts is a saturating, not proportional, function of neural drive.

  7. 7. You average 9 stimulus repeats instead of 1. Assuming independent noise, the SNR improves by roughly…

    Why: Independent noise shrinks as σ/√N while the locked signal stays, so SNR grows as √N = √9 = 3×. That square-root scaling is why repeats give diminishing returns — and why correlated noise (drift, motion) that doesn't average as √N is handled separately by nuisance regression.

  8. 8. On a log–log neural power spectrum, the straight descending background line (the aperiodic 1/f component) is…

    Why: The aperiodic 1/f background is itself informative — its exponent/offset shift with excitation–inhibition balance, arousal, anaesthesia, and age (Donoghue et al., 2020). Conflating it with oscillatory peaks produces spurious "oscillation" effects; tools like specparam fit aperiodic + peaks precisely to keep them separate.

Reference

Key terms

Single-unit
The spiking of one isolated, spike-sorted neuron recorded by a microelectrode — the sharpest, rarest signal.
Multi-unit activity (MUA)
The blended spiking of several nearby cells; "normalized MUA" is its windowed envelope reduced to one number per electrode.
High-gamma
The ~70–150 Hz ECoG power band whose increase proxies local population firing on a millisecond timescale.
EEG / MEG
Scalp electrical potentials (EEG) and magnetic fields (MEG) from synchronized post-synaptic currents; non-invasive, ms-fast, cm-coarse.
Inverse problem
Recovering internal sources from external EEG/MEG sensors — ill-posed: infinitely many configurations fit the data, so priors are required.
Open / closed field
An open field (e.g. pyramidal cells with a long apical dendrite) has separated source and sink, producing a far-reaching dipole; a closed field (radially symmetric cells) cancels by symmetry and is nearly invisible to EEG/MEG.
Radial / tangential source
A dipole pointing out through the skull (gyral crown) vs parallel to it (sulcal wall). MEG sees mainly tangential sources; EEG sees both.
Magnetometer / gradiometer
MEG sensor types: a magnetometer reads the raw field (deep but noise-prone); a gradiometer reads the field's spatial gradient, cancelling distant noise and favoring superficial sources.
Aperiodic 1/f
The smooth, peak-free background of a power spectrum (power ∝ 1/fβ); its slope/offset track E/I balance, arousal and age, and must be separated from true oscillatory peaks.
Neurovascular coupling
The link by which local neural activity triggers a local increase in blood flow and oxygenation — the basis of fMRI.
BOLD / T2*
Blood-Oxygen-Level-Dependent signal: a T2*-weighted readout where activity flushes out paramagnetic deoxyhemoglobin, lengthening T2* and raising the signal.
BOLD nonlinearity
The compressive, saturating relation between stimulus drive and BOLD amplitude — brief events overshoot, long/intense ones saturate below a linear prediction.
HRF
Hemodynamic response function — the delayed, smoothed vascular curve peaking ~4–6 s after a neural event, with a post-stimulus undershoot.
Voxel
A 3D volume element (~1–3 mm) of an fMRI scan pooling ~10⁶ neurons, glia and vasculature; each reports its local BOLD timeseries.
Nyquist limit
Half the sampling rate — the highest frequency a digitized signal can represent; content above it aliases into spurious low frequencies unless removed first.
Re-referencing (bipolar / CAR)
Recomputing each channel relative to a chosen reference: bipolar = neighbor minus neighbor; common-average = minus the mean of all channels. It changes what each channel measures.
√N law
Averaging N repeats shrinks independent noise to σ/√N, so SNR grows as √N — quadrupling trials only doubles SNR (diminishing returns).
Spatial / temporal resolution
How finely a method localizes activity in space and resolves it in time — the two axes that trade off against each other and against invasiveness.
