The Language Brain — comprehension in context.

How a stream of air pressure becomes meaning: a left-lateralized fronto-temporal network climbs from sound to syntax to sense, predicting each word before it arrives — and modern language models turn out to speak its dialect.

01 · The system

A network built for language

There is a system in the human brain that lights up for language — and stays remarkably quiet for arithmetic, music, faces, or working-memory puzzles. It is not a single "speech area." It is a distributed, left-lateralized language network.

The classical story is the one every textbook tells: in the 1860s Paul Broca linked damaged left inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) to halting, effortful speech; a decade later Carl Wernicke tied posterior superior temporal damage to fluent-but-meaningless output. Two regions, one wire (the arcuate fasciculus) between them. It is a beautiful map — and too simple. The modern picture, built largely from functional MRI with individually-localized regions, is a set of interconnected patches that behave as one functional unit.

The decisive move came from Fedorenko, Behr & Kanwisher (2011): by contrasting sentences against pronounceable nonword lists within each individual subject, they isolated a network that responds to the demands of linguistic structure and meaning — and showed it is selective. The same voxels that fire for language barely budge for hard non-linguistic tasks (mental arithmetic, spatial reasoning, music). Language is dissociable from general-purpose cognition; it has its own real estate.

IFG
Inferior frontal gyrus — the region around classical Broca's area; structure & combinatorics.
pSTS/MTG
Posterior superior/middle temporal — lexical access & meaning (classical Wernicke territory).
ATL
Anterior temporal lobe — a hub for combining word meanings into phrases.

Anatomically the network spans the left frontal and temporal lobes, connected by two great fiber bundles: a dorsal pathway (arcuate / superior longitudinal fasciculus) carrying sound-to-articulation and structural information, and a ventral pathway (extreme capsule / inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus) carrying sound-to-meaning. Think dorsal = "how to say it / how it's built," ventral = "what it means."

◆ In the brain

Localizing the network per individual matters. Anatomy varies enough between people that group-averaged maps blur the network into nearby regions. The "sentences > nonwords" localizer recovers the same functional system in each subject — which is exactly why its selectivity could be measured cleanly.

Selective — and dissociable from the executive system

Selectivity is more than a curiosity; it is a dissociation. The language network sits right next to, but is functionally distinct from, the brain's domain-general multiple-demand (MD) system — the fronto-parietal machinery that ramps up for any hard task (arithmetic, working memory, spatial reasoning). Within the same individuals, the two systems can be cleanly separated: a task that taxes general cognition lights the MD network and barely touches the language network, and vice versa. The cleanest converging evidence is clinical: people with global aphasia — a devastated language network — can still play chess, do arithmetic, solve non-verbal puzzles, and reason about others' intentions. Conversely, severe executive impairment can spare grammatical comprehension. Language is not "general intelligence applied to words"; it is its own organ.

⟐ Going deeper · the selectivity argument

Why does selectivity matter so much? Because it constrains theory. A common intuition is that language is just thought "read aloud" — that to comprehend a sentence is to run general reasoning over words. But if the language network is robustly doubly dissociated from the multiple-demand system (Fedorenko and colleagues), then the computations that map sound to meaning are not the same computations that solve a logic puzzle. This is why a sentences->-nonwords localizer, run per subject, has become the field's gold standard: it pins the system functionally rather than anatomically, so claims about "the language network" survive the wide variation in where these regions sit between brains.

The dual-stream model of speech

The dorsal and ventral pathways in Figure 05.1 are not incidental wiring — they are the backbone of the modern replacement for the Broca/Wernicke picture: the dual-stream model of Hickok & Poeppel (2007). Early cortical speech processing in the superior temporal cortex is bilateral (both hemispheres parse the acoustic signal), and it then forks into two streams. The ventral stream — running forward through middle and inferior temporal cortex — maps sound onto meaning: it is the comprehension route, and it is largely bilateral. The dorsal stream — running back and up through the temporo-parietal junction (area Spt) to premotor and inferior-frontal cortex — maps sound onto articulation: it is a sensorimotor interface for speech production, and it is strongly left-lateralized.

This reframes the classics. "Broca's area" is not a speech-output box and "Wernicke's area" is not a comprehension box; rather, the dorsal stream provides the auditory-motor mapping needed to learn and produce speech, and the ventral stream carries comprehension. It explains why pure articulatory deficits and pure comprehension deficits dissociate, and why damage along the dorsal route impairs repetition (you can understand a word but not echo it) — a pattern the single-arc arcuate story never explained cleanly.

Quick check — why is "selective" a stronger claim than "active during language"?
Lots of regions are active while you read. "Selective" means the language network responds to linguistic structure and meaning but not to equally hard non-linguistic tasks (arithmetic, spatial working memory, music) — so it is dissociated from the domain-general multiple-demand system. Activity alone could be a generic difficulty signal; a double dissociation (sparing in aphasia vs sparing of language under executive load) is what shows language has its own dedicated machinery.
02 · Sound to sense

The comprehension hierarchy

A spoken sentence enters the ear as nothing but fluctuating air pressure. By the time it reaches understanding, the brain has climbed a whole ladder of representation — each rung a different transformation.

Speech first reaches auditory cortex on the upper bank of the temporal lobe, then spreads along the superior temporal gyrus (STG). A landmark result from Mesgarani et al. (2014), recording directly from human STG with electrodes, showed that local populations are tuned not to whole phonemes but to phonetic features — clusters like plosives (/p/, /t/, /k/), fricatives (/s/, /f/), and vowels organized by where and how they are produced. The STG is, in effect, a phonetic-feature detector bank: the first place where the continuous waveform is carved into the discrete contrasts of speech.

From features the cascade continues: features assemble into phonemes, phonemes into words (a lexical lookup that retrieves a stored meaning), words bind under syntax into phrases and clauses — who did what to whom — and structure resolves into semantics and discourse: a coherent, evolving model of the situation being described. Higher rungs lean more on the frontal and anterior-temporal parts of the network; lower rungs on auditory/STG. No rung is a clean module — they overlap and talk to each other constantly — but the gradient from acoustic to abstract is real.

03 · Nested clocks

A hierarchy of timescales

The same ladder shows up in time. Different cortical areas integrate information over different durations — a property called the temporal receptive window (TRW): the length of past input a region's current response depends on.

Hasson, Lerner, Honey and colleagues demonstrated this with a clever manipulation: take a real spoken story and scramble it at different grain sizes — shuffle the words, the sentences, or the paragraphs — then ask which brain regions still respond reliably. Early auditory areas respond identically no matter how you scramble: they only care about the last few hundred milliseconds, the scale of phonemes and syllables. Higher areas in the temporal and frontal lobes care about word and sentence context. And the highest areas — precuneus, angular gyrus, prefrontal cortex — only respond coherently when whole paragraphs of narrative are intact. They are accumulating meaning over tens of seconds.

So the cortex is a stack of nested integrators: short windows feeding longer ones feeding longer ones still. This is exactly the temporal counterpart of the spatial hierarchy in the visual system from Chapter 4 — and it is why a continuous story, not a list of isolated words, is needed to engage the long-window regions at all.

★ Key idea

There is one hierarchy, read two ways. Spatially it runs acoustic → lexical → semantic up the cortex; temporally it runs short windows → long windows. The brain represents "now" at every scale at once.

04 · Information & cost

Surprisal: the cost of a word

Not all words cost the brain the same. Three lexical properties reliably move neural and behavioral responses: length (longer words demand more processing), frequency (rare words are slower and costlier to retrieve), and — most powerfully — predictability in context.

Predictability is made precise by Claude Shannon's information theory. The surprisal of a word is the negative log of its probability given everything that came before:

Definition · surprisal

surprisal(w) = −log P(w | context)

A word the context makes certain carries little information and little surprisal; a word that is improbable carries a lot. Take the sentence "I take my coffee with milk and ___". If P(sugar) = 0.5, then surprisal = −log₂(0.5) = 1 bit — barely a ripple. If a listener hears the unlikely P(plutonium) = 1/1024, surprisal = −log₂(1/1024) = 10 bits — a jolt.

The brain's response scales with surprisal. The classic electrophysiological signature is the N400 — a negative-going EEG/MEG deflection peaking around 400 ms after word onset whose amplitude grows the harder the word is to integrate with its context. Hale's (2001) surprisal theory gave the linking hypothesis — processing difficulty ∝ surprisal — and a line of work since (Frank et al.; Willems et al.; Goldstein et al. 2022) has shown that surprisal estimated by language models predicts graded N400-like and BOLD responses to natural text. The brain is not passively waiting for each word; it is constantly betting on what comes next, and it pays in proportion to how wrong the bet was.

⚠ Watch out

Surprisal is about context-conditional probability, not raw frequency. A common word can be highly surprising in the wrong context ("milk and the…" is frequent but ungrammatical here), and a rare word can be perfectly predictable ("the speed of light"). The N400 tracks the conditional bet, not the dictionary count.

Two ERP signatures: the N400 and the P600

The N400 is one of two famous event-related potentials (ERPs) evoked by an unexpected word, and the contrast between them is one of the cleanest dissociations in the psycholinguistic literature. The N400 — a centro-parietal negativity peaking near 400 ms — indexes semantic fit / predictability: it grows when a word is hard to integrate with the meaning built so far. In "He spread his warm bread with socks," the word socks is semantically anomalous, and it elicits a large N400 (Kutas & Federmeier 2011). Crucially, the N400 is graded by cloze probability — the same continuum as surprisal — so it is the canonical neural readout of "how expected was this word."

The P600 — a later, posterior positivity around 600 ms — is a different animal. It indexes syntactic reanalysis / structural repair, not semantic fit. In "The cats won't eating the food," nothing is semantically odd — you know exactly what is meant — but the grammar is broken, and the brain mounts a P600 as it tries to reparse. The two can even pull apart on the same word: a sentence like "The hearty meal was devouring the kids" is semantically reversed but syntactically fine, and (famously) elicits a P600 rather than the expected N400 — a "semantic illusion" suggesting structure-building and meaning-fit are partly separate processes. N400 ≈ "does this word make sense here?"; P600 ≈ "can I still build a legal structure?"

Quick check — a sentence is grammatical but ends with a bizarre word. Which ERP grows?
The N400. It indexes semantic fit / predictability, so a meaning-violating-but-grammatical word (e.g. "…spread his bread with socks") enlarges the ~400 ms negativity. A P600 is the signature of a grammatical violation that forces structural reanalysis — different process, later, posterior, positive-going.
05 · The predicting brain

The brain predicts the next word

If the response scales with surprisal, the brain must already hold an expectation to be surprised against. The strong claim of predictive processing is that comprehension is fundamentally anticipatory: the language system continuously forecasts upcoming input and treats the actual word as a confirmation or a correction.

The most striking direct evidence comes from Goldstein et al. (2022, Nature Neuroscience), using intracranial recordings during natural story listening. They found neural signatures of the predicted word before it was spoken — a pre-onset prediction signal — followed, after the word arrived, by a response scaling with how surprising it turned out to be. The brain runs the same predict-then-update loop that powers an autoregressive language model: guess the next token, hear it, register the error, move on.

pre-onset
A prediction signal appears before the word is heard — the brain commits to a guess.
on-onset
When the word arrives, the response scales with surprisal — prediction error.
update
Context is revised and the next prediction is issued — the loop continues.

Surprisal vs entropy: cost looking back, uncertainty looking ahead

Surprisal is computed after the word arrives — it is −log P(actual word | context), the cost of the bet you just paid out. But the same probability distribution supports a second, forward-looking quantity. Before the next word arrives, the context defines a whole distribution P(· | context), and its entropy — H = −Σ P(w) log P(w) — measures how uncertain the upcoming word is. A sharply peaked distribution ("the speed of ___" → almost surely light) has low entropy: little lookahead uncertainty. A flat distribution ("she picked up the ___" → dozens of plausible objects) has high entropy: the brain is hedging across many candidates.

The distinction matters because the two are dissociable and both modulate neural responses. Surprisal is the retrospective processing cost of the word you got; entropy is the prospective uncertainty about the word you're waiting for (sometimes split further into next-word vs multi-step "entropy reduction"). The idea traces to Hale's (2001) information-theoretic parsing, and studies using model-derived estimates (Willems et al.; Lopopolo et al.; Frank) report that entropy and surprisal explain separable variance in brain activity during natural reading and listening — anticipatory engagement (entropy) on top of integration cost (surprisal).

⟐ Going deeper · why both, intuitively

Think of the brain as placing bets. Entropy is how spread out your chips are before the card is turned — when the field is wide open you must prepare more alternatives, which is itself effortful. Surprisal is how badly you lost after the card is turned — the prediction error you must correct. They can move oppositely: a context can be high-entropy (many plausible nouns) yet yield low surprisal if the actual word was one of the likely ones; or low-entropy yet high surprisal if the near-certain continuation fails to appear ("the speed of darkness"). Disentangling them is exactly why model-derived probability estimates — and LLMs in particular — became indispensable tools for psycholinguistics.

Quick check — "the speed of darkness" — is that high surprisal, high entropy, or both?
High surprisal, low entropy. The context "the speed of ___" is sharply peaked on light (low entropy — little lookahead uncertainty), so the brain confidently expects one word. When darkness arrives instead, the realized word had tiny probability, so −log P is large — a big surprisal / prediction error. Low entropy plus high surprisal is exactly the "confident-but-wrong" case.
06 · The encoding model, applied

Brains and language models, aligned

Here Chapter 1's encoding-model logic returns, now pointed at language. Take the contextual embedding a GPT-style model assigns to each word — the high-dimensional vector that captures the word in its specific context — and fit a single linear map from that vector to the recorded brain response. The result, found again and again, is that contextual embeddings linearly predict brain activity for the same words.

And the relationship is graded in a remarkable way: Schrimpf et al. (2021, PNAS) showed that the better a language model is at next-word prediction, the better its embeddings predict the brain — the same architectures that win at language modeling win at brain modeling. Caucheteux & King (2022) reinforced the result across fMRI and MEG; Goldstein et al. (2022) and Zada et al. (2024) showed brains and LLMs share a model-based linguistic geometry during natural comprehension. A linear readout — exactly the move this whole handbook is built on — is enough to translate between an LLM's representation and a brain's.

Why should an LLM speak the brain's dialect?

The convergence is not a coincidence of scale — it follows from a shared objective. Both systems are optimized (the brain by evolution and experience, the model by gradient descent) to predict the next word from context. Two predictors that are both good at the same prediction problem, trained on the same statistics of language, tend to arrive at similar internal representations — so a linear map suffices to read one out from the other. This is the predictive-processing reading of the alignment: the N400, the pre-onset prediction signals of Goldstein et al. (2022), and the LLM's logits are three windows onto the same next-word forecast.

⟐ Going deeper · scale, and where the analogy strains

The "better LM → better fit" curve is real but not unbounded. Antonello, Vaidya & Huth (2024) found brain-prediction keeps improving roughly log-linearly as model size grows across orders of magnitude — yet the most predictive layers are typically the middle ones, not the final next-word head, and gains eventually saturate well below the noise ceiling. So the shared objective explains why the representations align, but the fit is partial: LLMs capture much of the contextual-semantic variance a Mel spectrogram cannot, while leaving residual structure (and a debate about whether "next-word prediction" is the brain's objective or merely a good proxy for it). Caution: correlation between two predictors is evidence of shared computation, not proof the brain is a transformer.

07 · The continuous story

Why a story, not a word list

Everything above — the long timescales, the running prediction, the contextual embedding — depends on context. An isolated word has none. This is the case for the naturalistic paradigm: studying the brain with continuous, ecologically valid stimuli like a spoken story.

A real narrative engages the full language network in its natural mode: it drives every rung of the comprehension hierarchy at once, lets long-window regions accumulate meaning, and gives the predictive machinery a genuine context to predict against. It is also reproducible in a powerful way. Hasson et al. (2004) introduced inter-subject correlation (ISC): play the same story (or film) to many people and correlate their brain time-courses with one another. Where responses are reliably shared across listeners, the stimulus — not idiosyncratic wandering thought — is driving the brain. ISC turns "is this region tracking the narrative?" into a measurable quantity, and it is high throughout the language network during story listening.

◈ In this project

Two of neuro-autoresearch's datasets are exactly this naturalistic story-listening paradigm: Le Petit Prince (the same audiobook recorded in fMRI and MEG) and the "Podcast" ECoG recording (intracranial iEEG while patients listened to a 30-minute story). In both, the harness triggers on each spoken Word and the held-out test_corr of a ridge readout is the reward.

But here is the honest catch — and the bridge to the next chapter. The stimulus feature the harness currently hands the model is the Mel spectrogram at the word: a low-level acoustic snapshot. That captures the bottom rungs of the ladder (the spectro-temporal energy, the cues to phonetic structure) but carries no lexical semantics and no context — exactly the information surprisal and contextual embeddings encode. So a Mel-only model can do well on acoustic/phonetic variance and will hit a ceiling on the meaning-driven, predictive responses that dominate the higher language network.

That gap is the opening for richer features — most pointedly the contextual LLM embeddings of Figure 05.5. Swapping or augmenting the acoustic stimulus with model-based linguistic features is precisely how an encoding model climbs from sound into meaning. Chapter 6 takes up that thread directly.

Key terms

Language network
A left-lateralized, fronto-temporal system (IFG, posterior STS/MTG, ATL) that responds selectively to linguistic structure and meaning — dissociable from general-purpose cognition.
Multiple-demand system
A domain-general fronto-parietal network that ramps up for any hard task; doubly dissociated from the language network (Fedorenko).
Dual-stream model
Hickok & Poeppel's account: a bilateral ventral stream (sound→meaning) and a left-lateralized dorsal stream (sound→articulation), replacing the box-and-arrow Broca/Wernicke map.
STG
Superior temporal gyrus — auditory/speech cortex where the waveform is carved into phonetic features; the entry point of the comprehension hierarchy.
Phonetic features
Sub-phonemic dimensions (plosive, fricative, vowel, place/manner of articulation) to which STG populations are tuned (Mesgarani et al. 2014).
Temporal receptive window
The duration of past input a region's current response depends on — short in early auditory cortex, tens of seconds in high-level narrative regions.
Surprisal
The information content of a word, −log P(word | context); the brain's response scales with it.
Entropy
−Σ P(w) log P(w) of the next-word distribution — prospective uncertainty (how spread-out the upcoming-word bets are), distinct from and complementary to surprisal.
N400
A negative EEG/MEG deflection ~400 ms after word onset whose amplitude grows with semantic surprisal / integration difficulty.
P600
A later posterior positivity (~600 ms) indexing syntactic reanalysis / structural repair — dissociable from the semantic N400.
Predictive processing
The view that comprehension is anticipatory — the brain forecasts upcoming words and treats input as confirmation or prediction error (pre-onset signals; Goldstein et al. 2022).
Contextual embedding
A language model's high-dimensional vector for a word in its context; these linearly predict brain responses, and better LMs predict better.
Naturalistic paradigm
Studying the brain with continuous, ecologically valid stimuli (a spoken story) rather than isolated, decontextualized probes.
Inter-subject correlation
Correlating different listeners' brain time-courses to the same stimulus; high ISC means the stimulus is reliably driving the response (Hasson et al. 2004).
