I
The naturalistic paradigm

Why read the brain a story?

For decades, the language sciences leaned on isolated stimuli — a single word flashed on a screen, a lone sentence judged for grammar. Clean, controllable, and nothing at all like how language is actually lived.

Real comprehension is continuous. Meaning accumulates across a narrative: a word lands in the shadow of the words before it, a sentence resolves a suspense built three sentences earlier. To study the language network as it truly works, you let a person simply listen to a story — and you record the brain as the plot unfolds, word by word, second by second.

That is the naturalistic paradigm, and its most beloved stimulus is Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s Le Petit Prince. The Le Petit Prince language-neuroscience project — led by Jixing Li with Jonathan Brennan, John Hale, Christophe Pallier and colleagues — recorded dozens of listeners hearing the same audiobook in their native language, then released the time-aligned recordings as an open corpus. Because the stimulus is fixed but the imaging can vary, the very same passages can be observed through completely different instruments.

A story is not a luxury — it is the experiment. It drives the full hierarchy of language processing in its natural context, and it gives every analysis a common ground truth: the exact same words, in the exact same order, every time.

✦ A note on the stimulus
Le Petit Prince
Saint-Exupéry’s 1943 novella — short, warm, and translated into hundreds of languages — which makes it an ideal multilingual stimulus: the same narrative, the same plot beats, available in every listener’s native tongue.
Naturalistic / ecological
A stimulus resembling real-world experience (a continuous spoken narrative) rather than an artificial, decontextualised probe (an isolated word or sentence).
II
WHERE · WHEN

Two windows on the same story

Hand the very same audiobook to two different scanners and the brain answers in two different alphabets. One tells you where in the cortex something happened; the other tells you when, to the millisecond. Neither alone is the whole truth — together they are stereo.

WHERE · fMRI

Blood follows thought

Li et al. · Scientific Data 2022 · NEURO_DATASET=text

“…an elephant inside a boa constrictor.”

fMRI watches the BOLD signal — blood-oxygenation. Active neurons demand oxygen, and the bloodstream answers. By tracking that oxygen flux across millimetre-scale voxels, fMRI builds a whole-brain map of where the story is being processed. The price: blood is slow, so one full volume arrives only every couple of seconds.

WHEN · MEG

The flicker of current

Bel et al. · 2026 · ds007523 · NEURO_DATASET=meg

«…un serpent boa qui digère un éléphant.»

MEG listens to the faint magnetic fields thrown off by neuronal currents themselves. There is no sluggish blood in the loop — the field appears almost the instant neurons fire, so MEG resolves when to the millisecond. The trade-off: magnetic fields blur on their way to the sensors, so the spatial picture is softer.

III
The hemodynamic catch

Why fMRI arrives five seconds late

fMRI never sees a neuron fire. It sees the blood that arrives afterwards — and blood is slow.

When a population of neurons activates, the local demand for oxygenated blood rises, swells, and subsides over several seconds. That sluggish wave is the hemodynamic response function (HRF). Even for an instantaneous burst of neural activity, the BOLD signal it produces does not peak immediately — it climbs gradually and crests roughly five seconds later, then dips below baseline before recovering.

This is exactly why the harness treats the two modalities differently. For fMRI it decouples the windows: the stimulus is measured at the word itself, but the neural target is read from a later window shifted forward to catch the HRF peak (start = 2.0 s, duration = 4.0 s). The blood’s delay never leaks into the stimulus features.

For MEG there is no blood in the loop — the magnetic field appears essentially the moment neurons fire — so the two windows coincide: stimulus and neural response are both read from the same [0, 0.5] s epoch after each word.

fMRI · decoupled

Stimulus window at the word · neural window shifted ~2–6 s later to meet the BOLD peak. Two clocks, deliberately offset.

MEG · coincident

Stimulus and neural windows are the same [0, 0.5] s epoch. One clock — the response is already there.

IV
What the brain is doing

From air pressure to meaning

A spoken sentence enters the ear as nothing but fluctuating air pressure. By the time it reaches comprehension, the brain has climbed a whole ladder of representation — each rung a different transformation, each handled by overlapping regions of the cortex.

01 · sound
Acoustics
Raw spectro-temporal energy — pitch, loudness, the envelope of speech.
→
02 · units
Phonemes
The contrastive speech sounds that distinguish boa from bow.
→
03 · lexicon
Words
Sound clusters mapped to stored meanings — the lexical lookup.
→
04 · structure
Syntax
Words bound into phrases and clauses — who did what to whom.
→
05 · sense
Meaning
Structure resolved into a coherent, evolving model of the story.

Much of this work falls to the brain’s language network — a left-lateralised system spanning the superior and middle temporal lobe and the inferior frontal gyrus (the region around classical Broca’s area). A naturalistic story exercises every rung of the ladder at once, in context, exactly as ordinary listening does.

When researchers model these responses, three word-level properties reliably move the needle:

len
Word length — longer words demand more processing.
freq
Frequency — rare words are slower and costlier to retrieve than common ones.
−log p
Surprisal — how unpredictable a word is in context; the unexpected costs more.

A useful intuition for our setting: a model that captures the right structure in the stimulus should produce activations that linearly predict these neural responses — which is precisely the test the harness applies.

V
The encoding model

How neuro-autoresearch reads the story

The harness frames the whole problem as an encoding model: take the stimulus, push it through a fixed model to get activations, and ask how well those activations predict the recorded brain. The model is never trained — only a simple ridge regression on top is fit, and that fit is the evaluation.

Concretely, each spoken Word triggers a segment. The stimulus features X are the Mel spectrogram of the audio at that word, time-averaged into a per-word vector. The target Y is the recorded brain — the HRF-delayed BOLD window for fMRI, the coincident [0, 0.5] s sensor window for MEG. The split is a contiguous temporal hold-out: the latest fifth of the story by word onset is the test set, so time-adjacent words never straddle the train/test line.

A model proposes some fixed transform of the spectrogram; the ridge readout learns the best linear map from its activations to the brain; and the held-out test_corr — the mean per-target Pearson correlation on words the readout never saw — is the reward to climb.

two stories, one harness
# fMRI — Le Petit Prince, English listener EN057 (HRF-decoupled windows)
NEURO_DATASET=text python -m harness.run

# MEG — Le Petit Prince, French audiobook, sub-26 (coincident windows)
NEURO_DATASET=meg  python -m harness.run

Each run evaluates the current model once and appends a single row to the per-study CSV — one row, one model, one iteration in an AlphaEvolve-style loop that keeps proposing never-trained models and keeps the ones that read the brain a little better.
