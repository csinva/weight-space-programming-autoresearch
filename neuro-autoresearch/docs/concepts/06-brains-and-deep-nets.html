Chapter 06 · Synthesis 06

Brains & Deep Networks

The leading predictive models of the visual brain are deep neural networks. The strangest part of the story is that you can throw away the training — and an untrained, hand-built model still predicts cortex. This final chapter ties the whole handbook to the thesis of neuro‑autoresearch: design a fixed, never-trained model that beats a trained AlexNet.

You'll understand

  1. How convolutional nets converged with the ventral visual stream — and why goal-driven DNNs became the best models of cortex.
  2. The encoding-model bridge: layer activations → linear readout → predicted voxels, and the hierarchical correspondence early↔early, deep↔high.
  3. The twist: why even random-weight networks yield brain-like features — and exactly where they fall short.
  4. The untrained model zoo you can hand-design — random conv features, Gabor/steerable pyramids, scattering transforms, HMAX — and why they are natural early-visual baselines.
  5. "Architecture vs learning" as a dial — what wiring fixes for free, and what experience (shape vs texture bias) must sculpt.
  6. Why "predicts well" is not "works the same," the identifiability problem, and how to compare models to brains honestly.

01 — The convergence

Two histories that turned out to be one

Deep convolutional networks were not reverse-engineered from the brain by accident. Their core ideas were lifted, generation by generation, straight out of visual neuroscience. The remarkable payoff came decades later: those same networks, trained only to recognize objects, turned out to be the best predictive models we have of the primate visual cortex.

In the 1960s Hubel & Wiesel recorded from cat and monkey primary visual cortex (V1) and found two cell types: simple cells tuned to oriented edges at a specific position, and complex cells tuned to the same orientation but tolerant to its exact location. A "detect-an-oriented-feature, then pool over position" motif — local filtering followed by spatial pooling — was the seed of everything that followed.

That motif was made explicit in Fukushima's Neocognitron (1980), which stacked "S-cells" (simple, feature-detecting) and "C-cells" (complex, pooling) into a deep hierarchy. LeCun's convolutional networks (late 1980s–1990s) added gradient learning and weight-shared convolutions, giving us LeNet. Then in 2012 Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton's AlexNet — five convolutional layers, three fully-connected layers, ReLU, dropout, trained on ImageNet with GPUs — cut the ImageNet error roughly in half and started the deep-learning era.

  • 1980Fukushima · NeocognitronS-cell / C-cell hierarchy: the architecture of a CNN, before backprop.

The scientific turn came in 2014. Yamins, Hong, Cadieu, Solomon, Seibert & DiCarlo (PNAS 2014) showed that networks optimized for the goal of object recognition — not fit to neural data — automatically produced the best available predictions of single-unit responses in monkey IT cortex. In parallel, Khaligh-Razavi & Kriegeskorte (2014) showed (via representational similarity analysis) that a supervised deep net explained the representational geometry of human and monkey IT far better than dozens of earlier models. The phrase that stuck was goal-driven deep network: train for a task, and brain-like representations fall out as a by-product. The community later built Brain-Score (Schrimpf et al. 2018/2020) to rank models against cortex on a common yardstick.

In the brain

The ventral stream — V1 → V2 → V4 → inferotemporal (IT) cortex — is the "what" pathway (Chapter 04). It is largely feedforward on the first sweep, builds increasingly abstract and position-tolerant representations, and culminates in IT, where neurons respond to object categories and where you can linearly read out object identity. That feedforward, increasingly-invariant hierarchy is exactly what a deep CNN approximates.

02 — The bridge

The encoding model, applied to a deep net

Chapter 01 built the encoding model: take features of a stimulus, fit a linear map from those features to each voxel or neuron, and score how well it predicts held-out responses. Chapter 06's whole idea is to make those features the activations of a layer in a deep network. The model supplies the representation; a ridge regression supplies the (only) learned mapping into the brain.

Hierarchical correspondence: the signature result

Fit the encoding readout from each layer to each brain area and a clean diagonal appears: early layers best predict early visual areas (V1/V2), middle layers best predict V4, and deep layers best predict IT / high-level regions. The depth of the layer that best predicts a region tracks that region's position in the cortical hierarchy. This is the single most reproduced finding in the field — and it is the reason the encoding bridge counts as evidence, not coincidence.

Why AlexNet fc7 is a strong visual-cortex feature space

AlexNet's penultimate fully-connected layer, fc7, is a 4096-dimensional vector summarizing "what object-ish content is in this image" just before the final category decision. It is abstract enough to carry semantic, category-level structure, yet not collapsed to 1000 class logits. Empirically it reads out well across mid- and high-level visual cortex, which is why a frozen ImageNet AlexNet (fc7) is the per-region bar our project tries to beat.

In this project

neuro-autoresearch is exactly this bridge, run as a search. A proposed model is a plain callable X (N, D_in) → activations (N, D_out); the harness fits a ridge readout from those activations to recorded brain activity and reports test_corr = mean per-target Pearson r on a held-out split. On TVSD macaque spiking the target is IT neurons; on NSD it is human fMRI voxels in a chosen region (V1…ventral). The only thing trained is that ridge — which is the evaluation.

03 — The twist

Untrained networks already see a little

Here is the result that makes this project possible. Take a convolutional network and do not train it — leave the weights at random initialization. Push images through. The activations are still useful: read them out with the same ridge and they predict visual cortex surprisingly well, especially in early and mid-level areas. The architecture itself — local convolution, nonlinearity, pooling, normalization — is doing a lot of the work, before any learning happens.

Why should random weights help at all? Because a convolution with random filters is, in effect, a bank of random local feature detectors applied identically everywhere, and a random projection of a structured input preserves a lot of its structure. This is the random features idea: Rahimi & Recht (2007) showed random projections can approximate powerful kernels; Jarrett, Kavukcuoglu, Ranzato & LeCun (2009) asked "what is the best multi-stage architecture for object recognition?" and found that with the right nonlinearity and pooling, random-filter stages were shockingly competitive. The architectural priors of a CNN are strong enough that even at initialization the network computes Gabor-like, edge-sensitive, locally-pooled features — the very things V1 and V2 care about. Later work (e.g. Cadena et al., Geiger and colleagues) sharpened where untrained features do and do not hold up.

Watch out

"Untrained networks predict the brain" is true but easy to over-read. The honest statement: untrained / random-weight nets are competitive in early and mid-level visual areas, where representations are dominated by local image statistics that the architecture already captures. In high-level, category-selective areas (IT, FFA, PPA, EBA, VWFA) trained networks pull clearly ahead — those semantic, category-tuned representations are learned, not handed over by wiring alone.

In this project

This is the entire premise. Because architecture alone buys you early vision, a hand-designed, never-trained model can plausibly match or beat a trained AlexNet in V1–V3 — and empirically it does. The high-level regions are the open frontier: can you write, by hand, the right priors to capture FFA/PPA-style selectivity without any backprop? That is what the search is for.

Quick check — why do oriented, Gabor-like filters appear in conv1 before any training?
Because the architecture, not the data, produces them. A convolution is a bank of small local filters applied identically everywhere (translation equivariance + locality), so a random filter is already a localized, oriented, band-pass detector — exactly the kind of edge/orientation tuning V1 simple cells have. Training tidies and selects these filters, but the qualitative Gabor-like structure is handed over for free by the wiring. This is why random-weight nets predict V1/V2 well but need learning to capture category-selective IT/FFA.
04 — The untrained zoo

A toolbox of training-free feature hierarchies

If architecture alone carries early vision, the question becomes practical: which training-free architectures should you hand-design? Decades of work — much of it predating deep learning — built feature hierarchies that capture early/mid visual cortex with no fitted weights at all. These are the natural baselines a model in this project can write down directly in code, and several were biologically inspired from the start.

Four members of the zoo

Each of these is a deep, fixed feature hierarchy you can write down without a training set — and each captures a real chunk of early/mid visual cortex.

Random convolutional features

Convolution + nonlinearity + pooling with random filters. Jarrett et al. (2009) found that with the right rectification and local normalization, random-filter stages were startlingly competitive at recognition; Saxe et al. (2011) showed random-weight CNNs already have strong object-classification structure, isolating the contribution of architecture. The lesson: convolution + pooling is most of the prior.

Gabor & steerable pyramids

A multi-scale, multi-orientation bank of Gabor filters (Daugman 1985) or a steerable pyramid (Simoncelli & Freeman 1995) is a direct model of V1 simple-cell tuning. Gabor-wavelet pyramids underlie classic fMRI V1 encoding models (Kay et al. 2008) and remain a strong, fully hand-specified early-visual baseline.

Scattering transforms

The wavelet scattering transform (Bruna & Mallat 2013) cascades wavelet convolutions, a modulus nonlinearity, and averaging — a training-free deep network that is provably stable to deformations and translation-invariant. It is the cleanest example of a hierarchy whose invariances are designed, not learned, and it gives brain-like early/mid features for free.

HMAX

HMAX (Riesenhuber & Poggio 1999; Serre et al. 2007) alternates S (template-matching, tuning) and C (max-pooling, invariance) layers — a direct computational reading of simple/complex cells. It was a leading model of rapid object recognition and the ventral stream for a decade before trained deep nets, with most of its filters fixed by design.

Why are these the natural baselines? Because all four share the same skeleton — localized filtering → nonlinearity → pooling, stacked — which is precisely the Hubel-&-Wiesel motif and precisely what an untrained CNN computes. They make explicit the structure of natural-image statistics (orientation, scale, local energy, position tolerance) that dominates early and mid visual cortex, so they predict V1–V4 well with zero fitting. What they cannot supply is the semantic, category-selective content of IT/FFA/PPA — that is the part learning has to install.

Going deeper

There is a precise reason scattering and HMAX work without training: invariance can be engineered rather than learned. Translation invariance falls out of spatial pooling/averaging; scale and small-deformation stability fall out of the wavelet cascade (Mallat's deformation-stability theorem). A trained CNN discovers similar invariances by optimizing a task, but they are not free lunches the architecture forbids — they are properties you can also build in. That is why "untrained" early vision is not "assumption-free": the convolutional + pooling prior already encodes locality, translation equivariance, and multi-scale structure. The open question this project pushes on is how far up the hierarchy hand-designed invariances and priors can substitute for learned ones.

In this project

Every member of this zoo is a legal model.py: a plain callable X (N, D_in) → activations (N, D_out) with weights written directly in code and no optimizer or backprop. A Gabor pyramid, a steerable-pyramid energy model, a one- or two-layer scattering transform, or an HMAX-style S/C stack are all things you can hand-design and hand to the ridge. They are the project's strongest known untrained baselines for V1–V3 — the launch pad for trying to beat trained AlexNet there, and the raw material to extend toward the high-level frontier.

Quick check — the scattering transform and HMAX are both called "untrained." What part of each is genuinely fixed, and where does their invariance come from?
Their filters/templates are fixed by design (oriented wavelets in scattering; Gabor-like S1 templates in HMAX), and crucially their invariance is built in by pooling — modulus + averaging in scattering, max-pooling in HMAX's C layers — not discovered by gradient descent. A trained CNN would learn comparable invariances; these models engineer them. That is exactly why they capture early/mid cortex (dominated by local statistics + position tolerance) for free, but still fall short of learned category-selective IT representations.
04 — Architecture vs learning

A dial, not a switch

The twist sharpens into a real scientific question: how much of a cortical representation is fixed by wiring and architecture, and how much is sculpted by experience and learning? The encoding results suggest the answer is not the same everywhere along the visual hierarchy — it is a dial that slides from "architecture suffices" to "learning required" as you climb from V1 to IT.

Two caveats keep this honest. First, "architecture" here smuggles in a lot: the convolutional prior is a strong, evolved-and-engineered assumption about natural images (translation equivariance, locality, multi-scale pooling). Calling early vision "untrained" does not mean "assumption-free" — it means the assumptions live in the wiring rather than in fitted weights. Second, the dividing line is gradual and depends on the readout, the dataset, and the network; treat the dial as a robust qualitative trend, not a sharp boundary.

What learning actually installs: shape vs texture

If architecture gives you edges and pooling for free, what does training add? A sharp, controlled answer comes from Geirhos et al. (2019). They built cue-conflict images — a cat's shape rendered in an elephant's texture — and asked what an ImageNet-trained CNN reports. Humans overwhelmingly say "cat" (they classify by shape); standard CNNs say "elephant" (they classify by texture). Texture bias is a learned property — a consequence of what ImageNet training rewards — and retraining on a stylized dataset shifts the network toward human-like shape bias and improves robustness. The point for us: the content of high-level representations, the very thing that distinguishes a trained net from an untrained one, is exactly the part shaped by the training objective and data — and it is not always brain-like by default.

Key idea

Architecture and learning are complementary sources of the same representation. Early visual cortex is well-approximated by the structure of a convolutional net; high-level, semantic, category-selective cortex additionally needs the content that task learning installs — and that content (e.g. shape vs texture bias) is contingent on the objective and data, not guaranteed to be brain-like. Where exactly the hand-off happens — and how much of "learning" can be replaced by smarter hand-designed priors — is an empirical question you can actually run.

05 — The project's thesis

Hand-design a model that beats trained AlexNet

Everything above sets up one concrete, falsifiable goal. Take a fixed, never-trained model — weights written directly in code, no optimizer, no backprop — and, read out with the same ridge regression, make its held-out test_corr exceed that of a frozen, ImageNet-trained AlexNet (fc7) on a given brain region. If architecture really carries early vision, this should be achievable in V1–V3. In high-level regions, it is an open challenge.

Two rules keep the comparison meaningful and the result interpretable. The model is never trained: no gradient ever touches its parameters; if you want a weight, you write it. And only the ridge readout is fit — the same readout for every model — so any change in test_corr is attributable to the model's representation, not to extra fitting. A high train_corr with low test_corr just means the readout overfit; the held-out number is what counts (and small differences, below ~0.005, are noise).

In the brain

The NSD target is not the raw image-evoked signal but the HRF-peak fMRI response: the blood-oxygen response peaks several seconds after a stimulus, so the harness averages the peak TRs in a window around ~5 s post-onset into one value per voxel. Aligning to that lag is what turns noise into signal — a trained AlexNet scores ~0.006 at zero offset and ~0.32 at the HRF peak.

06 — The autoresearch loop

Searching the space of fixed models

You don't have to hand-tune one model forever. neuro-autoresearch runs an AlphaEvolve-style loop: an agent proposes a model.py, the fixed harness evaluates it once and logs a single row, and the agent reads the score and the history to propose a better model — hill-climbing toward (and past) the AlexNet bar, using test_corr as the reward.

In this project

Each python -m harness.run evaluates the current model once and appends a single row (iteration, model_name, idea, summary, test_corr, train_corr, gap, best_so_far, …, corr_<region>) to the study CSV — an append-only history of every model tried. The reward narrows to one region via NEURO_ROI (default ventral), while every region's predictivity is still logged. For unattended hill-climbing the loop runs nonstop under a launcher, bounded by a wall-clock timeout. The same fixed harness scores image models on macaque IT and language models on the Podcast ECoG target.

07 — Epistemology

Prediction is not mechanism

A model that predicts cortex well has earned something real — but not the right to claim it works the way the brain works. The most important habit in this whole enterprise is keeping "predicts the responses" and "implements the same computation" firmly apart.

A few guardrails worth keeping. Encoding vs RSA: the encoding model fits a linear map from model features to each unit and scores prediction; representational similarity analysis (RSA) instead compares the geometry of representations (the matrix of pairwise stimulus distances) without fitting a mapping. They probe related but distinct things; agreement across both is stronger evidence than either alone. The readout matters: a flexible linear readout can mask differences — "linearly decodable from the model" is a weaker claim than "represented the same way." Goals and limits: model–brain comparison is most useful for adjudicating between competing hypotheses, not for declaring a model "correct."

The honest framing for this research program is neuroconnectionism (Doerig et al., 2023): treat deep networks as explicit, testable hypotheses about brain computation — to be probed, compared, and falsified — rather than as finished theories. Good prediction is the start of an explanation, not its end. The danger to avoid is over-claiming: that a high test_corr means the brain "is" the model.

Watch out

Resist three over-reads: (1) "untrained nets predict cortex, so learning doesn't matter" — false; learning dominates high-level vision. (2) "high r, so the model is the brain's algorithm" — prediction ≠ mechanism. (3) "more parameters / a fancier readout, so a better model" — a flexible readout can launder representational differences into a good score. Keep the readout fixed and the held-out metric honest, exactly as the harness does.

08 — The identifiability problem

Many models, near-identical predictions

Prediction-is-not-mechanism has a quantitative twin that bites even harder: the data are often underdetermined. A whole family of genuinely different models can predict the same recordings to within noise, so the encoding score alone cannot tell them apart. This is the identifiability problem, and it is the reason metrics and benchmarks have to be chosen — and read — with care.

Metrics matter: encoding r vs RSA vs CKA

Because no single number settles a mechanistic claim, the field uses several model–brain comparison metrics, and they can disagree:

  • Encoding correlation (r) — fit a linear map from model features to each unit and score held-out prediction (this project's test_corr). Sensitive to which features are linearly present; depends on the readout's flexibility.
  • Representational similarity analysis (RSA) — compare the geometry of representations (the matrix of pairwise stimulus distances) with no fitted mapping (Kriegeskorte et al. 2008). Mapping-free, but coarse.
  • Centered kernel alignment (CKA) — a similarity index between two representations that is invariant to rotation and isotropic scaling (Kornblith et al. 2019). Popular for layer-to-layer and model-to-brain comparison, but itself sensitive to choices (e.g. linear vs RBF kernel) and known to be gameable.

Two representations can look aligned under one metric and divergent under another. Agreement across metrics is far stronger evidence than a single high number — and any one metric, optimized hard enough, invites benchmark-gaming: a model can climb a leaderboard by exploiting the metric's blind spots rather than by becoming more brain-like. Standardized benchmarks like Brain-Score (Schrimpf et al. 2018/2020) make models comparable, but a ranking is a measurement under a fixed metric, not a proof of mechanism.

Going deeper

Identifiability is why neuroconnectionism (Doerig et al. 2023) frames deep nets as hypotheses to be falsified, not theories to be confirmed. The productive move is not "find the model with the highest r" but design experiments and metrics that separate models that the encoding score ties — stimuli that two candidate models predict to disagree on, lesion/perturbation tests, cross-metric agreement (encoding ∧ RSA ∧ CKA), and out-of-distribution generalization. A tie band (small differences below ~0.005 are noise) is not a nuisance to round away; it is the honest statement that the data, as collected, cannot yet adjudicate.

Beyond vision: the same logic in language cortex

None of this is vision-specific. Chapter 05 ran the identical bridge in the language domain: take the internal activations of a large language model as it reads text, fit a linear readout to recorded language-cortex responses, and score held-out prediction. The striking parallel result — that next-word-prediction-trained LLMs are among the best predictors of language regions (e.g. Schrimpf et al. 2021; Goldstein et al. 2022; Caucheteux & King 2022) — is the same goal-driven story as vision: optimize a network for a competence (recognition, prediction) and brain-aligned representations emerge as a by-product. And the same caveats apply unchanged — prediction ≠ mechanism, metrics matter, the data are underdetermined.

In this project

The harness builds identifiability discipline in directly. Only the ridge is fit, the readout is fixed across models, the held-out split is honest, and small differences (below ~0.005) are treated as noise — a tie band, not a verdict. The append-only history logs every model's idea and summary so ties are visible rather than silently resolved. And the same fixed bridge scores vision models on macaque IT and language models on Podcast ECoG — one encoding-model logic across modalities.

Quick check — your hand-built scattering model and a frozen AlexNet both score r = 0.32 on V1, within the noise band. What can you, and can you not, conclude?
You can conclude that V1's image-evoked responses are largely captured by local, oriented, multi-scale, pooled features — both models supply those, which is consistent with architecture/statistics carrying early vision. You cannot conclude that V1 "is" either model, nor that learning is irrelevant in general (it dominates higher up), nor that the two compute V1 the same way: the tie is an identifiability statement — the encoding score does not separate them. Separating them needs a different probe (RSA/CKA agreement, perturbations, stimuli on which the two models predict different things).

Key terms

Goal-driven DNN
A deep network optimized for a task (e.g. object recognition), not fit to neural data — yet whose representations end up being the best available predictors of cortex (Yamins & DiCarlo, 2014).
Brain-Score
A standardized benchmark (Schrimpf et al., 2018/2020) that scores and ranks models on how well they predict primate visual cortex across regions.
AlexNet fc7
The 4096-dimensional penultimate fully-connected layer of AlexNet — a strong, semantic visual-cortex feature space and the per-region bar this project tries to beat.
Random features
Features from random (untrained) projections or filters; surprisingly powerful because architecture imposes structure even without learning (Rahimi & Recht, 2007; Jarrett et al., 2009).
Architectural prior
The assumptions baked into a network's wiring — local convolution, nonlinearity, pooling, normalization — that yield Gabor-like, brain-like early features before any training.
Task optimization
Learning driven by a behavioral goal; what installs the semantic, category-selective representations that dominate high-level visual cortex.
Architecture-vs-learning
The dial from "wiring suffices" (early vision) to "learning required" (high-level, category-selective vision) — the scientific question the project probes.
Prediction vs mechanism
A high encoding score (good prediction) does not prove the model computes the way the brain does (same mechanism); models are testable hypotheses, not finished theories (neuroconnectionism; Doerig et al., 2023).
Untrained model zoo
Training-free feature hierarchies you can hand-design — random conv features, Gabor/steerable pyramids, wavelet scattering, HMAX — natural baselines for early/mid visual cortex (Jarrett 2009; Saxe 2011; Bruna & Mallat 2013; Riesenhuber & Poggio 1999).
Shape vs texture bias
ImageNet-trained CNNs classify by texture where humans use shape (Geirhos et al. 2019) — a learned, objective-dependent property, evidence that what training installs need not be brain-like.
Identifiability
Many mechanistically distinct models can predict the same data within noise, so the encoding score is underdetermined; cross-metric agreement (encoding r, RSA, CKA) is stronger evidence than any single number.

  1. 1. Yamins et al. (2014) found that the best predictors of monkey IT responses were networks optimized for object recognition — not networks fit to neural data. Why is "optimized for the task, not the brain" the load-bearing phrase?

If the net had been fit to IT directly, a good score would be unsurprising and uninformative; the surprise (and the science) is that a task objective alone produces the best IT predictions.

It is what turns "a net predicts cortex" from coincidence into structured evidence. It holds (with shifts) for trained nets especially; untrained nets reproduce the early end of it.

Semantic, category-selective representations in IT/FFA/PPA are learned; trained nets win those. Option (a) and (c) are the classic over-reads.

  4. 4. Why are wavelet scattering transforms (Bruna & Mallat 2013) and HMAX (Riesenhuber & Poggio 1999) natural untrained baselines for early/mid cortex?

    Why: Both are deep, fixed hierarchies: oriented wavelet/Gabor filters → a modulus/max nonlinearity → pooling, repeated. Translation invariance comes from pooling/averaging and deformation stability from the wavelet cascade — built in, not learned. That is precisely why they capture the local-statistics-dominated early/mid stream with zero training. They are not pre-trained CNNs (b) and fit no weights (c).

  5. 5. Geirhos et al. (2019) showed standard ImageNet CNNs classify cue-conflict images by texture while humans use shape. What does this reveal about "architecture vs learning"?

    Why: Retraining on stylized images shifts the network toward shape bias, so the bias is a property of the training diet, not the wiring (ruling out a). It illustrates the dial's high-level end: learning supplies the content of high-level representations, and that content depends on the objective — it can diverge from human/brain processing.

  6. 6. Two mechanistically different models (a trained CNN and a hand-built scattering model) both score r ≈ 0.32 on V1, within the noise band. This is an example of the identifiability problem. What follows?

    Why: When competing models are statistically indistinguishable on the metric, prediction is underdetermined: a tie (or high r) constrains but does not identify the mechanism. Distinguishing them needs stimuli on which they predict different things, cross-metric agreement (RSA/CKA), or perturbation tests — not a bigger model (a) and not the conclusion of identical mechanism (c).

  7. 7. Encoding correlation, RSA, and CKA can disagree about how aligned a model and a brain region are. What is the right methodological response?

    Why: Each metric has blind spots (a flexible linear readout can launder differences; CKA is sensitive to kernel choice and is gameable). Optimizing one metric hard invites gaming. Cross-metric agreement (encoding ∧ RSA ∧ CKA) is the honest, more robust signal — not cherry-picking (a) or naive averaging (b).

  8. 8. The project's thesis is "beat a trained AlexNet." Given the architecture-vs-learning dial, where is this most achievable and where is it the open challenge?
