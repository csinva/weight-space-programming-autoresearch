How you build — and, more importantly, how you test — a model of the brain. The whole handbook rests on one deceptively simple idea: a good model of a brain region should predict its responses to stimuli it has never seen.

01 — The central question

What is "a model of the brain"?

Ask a neuroscientist whether they understand how some patch of cortex works, and you have asked a slippery question. A diagram with boxes and arrows feels like understanding. So does a story about "edge detectors" or "face cells." But stories are cheap, and the brain has humiliated a great many plausible stories. Systems neuroscience needed a criterion that a model either passes or fails — and it found one in prediction.

The operational definition is blunt: a model of a neural system is a function that takes a stimulus and outputs the system's response, and a good model predicts the response to novel, held-out stimuli. Not the stimuli you tuned it on — new ones. If your account of V1 can look at a photograph it has never been shown and say, in advance, how strongly each voxel will light up, and it is right, then in a precise and falsifiable sense you have captured something true about V1's computation. If it cannot, then however elegant the story, you do not yet understand the system.

This is the same move physics made centuries ago and the same move machine learning made recently: prediction on unseen data is the test of understanding. It converts vague claims ("this region represents faces") into a wager you can lose ("then this model should predict its response to these new faces"). Everything in this handbook — and every model the autoresearch loop ever proposes — is judged by that wager.

◆ Key idea

Prediction is the test of understanding. A model "understands" a brain region to exactly the degree that it can predict that region's responses to stimuli it was not fit on. Fit-quality on the training data proves nothing; generalization to held-out data is the only currency.

02 — Two directions

Encoding vs. decoding models

Brain and stimulus sit at two ends of an arrow, and you can read that arrow in either direction. Which direction you choose is not a detail — it changes what hypothesis you are actually testing.

An encoding model runs forward: from the stimulus (or features computed from it) to brain activity. It answers "given this image, what will the brain do?" A decoding model runs backward: from brain activity to a guess about the stimulus — "given this activity, what was the person looking at?" Decoding is what powers headline-grabbing "mind-reading" results; encoding is the quieter, stronger scientific instrument.

Why encoding is the stronger test

Here is the subtlety. A decoder can succeed for reasons that have nothing to do with how the brain represents anything. If faces and houses happen to drive even slightly different overall activity levels, a powerful decoder will exploit that statistical crumb and "read your mind" — without your having proposed any account of how faces are represented. Decoding asks "is the information present somewhere, recoverable somehow?" That is a real and useful question, but it is a question about the data, not a test of a theory.

An encoding model is generative in the scientific sense: you must commit, up front, to a specific hypothesis about what features the brain computes — edges? curvature? word meaning? — embody that hypothesis as an explicit function, and then let it predict every target. There is nowhere to hide. If your feature hypothesis is wrong, the predictions are wrong, region by region. This is why Naselaris, Kay, Prenger & Gallant (2011) frame encoding models as the primary tool for testing representational theories, and why Kriegeskorte & Douglas (2018) and van Gerven's tutorials place them at the center of computational systems neuroscience.

● In the brain

Encoding and decoding are formally linked: a fitted encoding model defines, via Bayes' rule, an optimal decoder. But the converse is weaker — a good decoder need not correspond to any honest model of the underlying representation. That asymmetry is exactly why a theory of the brain is built from encoding models.

03 — The architecture of a test

The two-stage encoding pipeline

Modern encoding models are built in two stages, and keeping them separate is the whole trick. Stage one is a feature model — a possibly deeply nonlinear function that turns a raw stimulus into a vector of features. Stage two is a linear mapping from those features to the predicted neural response.

The pipeline reads left to right: stimulus → feature model → linear readout → predicted response, which is then compared against the recorded response to produce a correlation. The division of labor is deliberate. The feature model carries the scientific hypothesis — "the brain cares about these properties of the input." The linear readout is just the dictionary that maps hypothesized features onto each specific neuron or voxel, accounting for the fact that every recording site weights the features differently.

The model is never trained; the readout fit is the evaluation.

04 — Why the readout stays linear

The linear readout hypothesis

It is tempting to make stage two a powerful deep decoder — surely that would predict better? It would. And that is precisely why we don't. The linearity of the readout is not a limitation we tolerate; it is the experiment.

The reasoning comes from DiCarlo & Cox (2007) and DiCarlo, Zoccolan & Rust (2012). A downstream neuron's basic operation is, to first approximation, a weighted sum of its inputs followed by a threshold. So information that a single downstream neuron could plausibly use is information that a linear reader can extract. We say such information is explicit or linearly accessible. If a feature space lets a linear map predict the neural response, those features have made the relevant structure explicit — the hard work is already done in the representation.

This reframes object recognition as untangling. Pixels of a face form a hopelessly curved, tangled manifold — no straight cut separates "face" from "not-face" in pixel space. A good visual hierarchy progressively warps that manifold until the categories become linearly separable. A linear readout therefore measures something deep: not whether the information is buried somewhere, but whether the features have untangled it into a form a real neuron could read. A deep nonlinear decoder would smuggle the untangling into stage two and let a lazy feature model pass — defeating the test.

How a flexible readout flatters a bad model

To see why this is not pedantry, run the thought experiment. Suppose your feature model is genuinely poor — say it just returns the raw pixels, or random projections of them. Bolt a deep nonlinear decoder onto stage two and let it train freely, and the score can climb sharply: the decoder learns the untangling the features failed to do, then maps the result onto the brain. The pipeline now reports a high number, but the number is a verdict on the decoder, not on the hypothesis you wanted to test. The flexibility of the readout has rescued a weak representation, and you have learned nothing about the brain.

Keeping the readout linear and regularized closes that escape hatch. A linear map cannot conjure structure that the features have not already made explicit — it can only reweight and sum what is handed to it. Regularization further forbids it from exploiting thousands of tiny, idiosyncratic feature directions to memorize the training targets. So the score is forced to reflect the geometry of the representation itself: did the features place same-response stimuli near each other along directions a weighted sum can read? That is exactly the linear-readout hypothesis, and it is why the test is meaningful rather than merely high.

⤇ Going deeper

The expressiveness of the readout is a dial on what you are measuring. At one extreme — nearest-neighbour or a deep decoder — you measure decodability: is the information present anywhere in the features? At the other — a fixed linear probe with no fitting — you measure raw alignment. Regularized linear regression sits in the scientifically useful middle: it asks whether the information is linearly accessible, the form a single downstream neuron could exploit (DiCarlo & Cox 2007; Kriegeskorte & Douglas 2018). Slide the dial toward more power and every model looks better — and every model looks more alike, so the test stops discriminating between hypotheses.

⚠ Watch out

"Why not just use a neural-net decoder for the readout?" Because then a high score would no longer tell you that your feature model captured the brain's computation — the decoder might have done the heavy lifting. Linearity pins the credit on the representation. Keep the readout linear and you keep the test honest.

Quick check — a colleague swaps the ridge readout for a 3-layer MLP and the score jumps. What have they actually shown?
That the information needed to predict the brain is present somewhere in the feature space — i.e. it is decodable. They have not shown that their feature model captured the brain's computation, because the MLP may have performed the untangling itself. The original question ("are these features what the brain makes explicit?") is now unanswerable: the credit is shared with the decoder.
05 — Fitting the readout

Ridge regression & the bias–variance trade-off

"Linear readout" means: find a weight matrix W so that ŷ = Wa best matches the recorded responses. The textbook way is ordinary least squares (OLS): pick the W that minimizes squared error on the training data. In real neural data, OLS quietly falls apart — and the fix, ridge regression, is one of the most important ideas on this page.

Three things break OLS in this setting. First, there are often many features — a feature vector can be thousands of dimensions, while a recording might have only a few thousand training samples, so the number of parameters p approaches or exceeds the number of samples n (p ≈ n or p > n). Second, features are typically collinear — correlated with one another — which makes the OLS solution wildly unstable: tiny wiggles in the data swing the weights enormously. Third, we fit thousands of targets at once (every voxel or electrode), and OLS will happily overfit each one.

Ridge regression adds an L2 penalty: instead of minimizing error alone, it minimizes error plus λ times the squared length of W. The penalty λ (a single positive number, chosen by cross-validation) pays a price for large weights, so the fit shrinks every coefficient toward zero. That deliberate shrinkage trades a little bias for a large cut in variance — and on noisy held-out data, the lower-variance solution wins, often by a lot.

⬢ In this project

Ridge is the entire stage-2 mechanism in the harness, and it is what makes a fixed model evaluable at all: with thousands of feature dimensions and thousands of voxels, OLS would be hopeless. The penalty λ is selected on the training split, the readout is fit once, and the resulting test_corr is the model's reward. Tune nothing else — the model's weights are frozen by definition.

05b — Choosing the regularizer

Why ridge, how λ is chosen, and banded ridge

Ridge is not the only way to tame a wide, collinear design matrix. So why this one? Because of what it does to the solution — and because it extends, almost for free, to the thing encoding models really need: combining several feature spaces honestly.

The natural alternatives all attack collinearity by first compressing the features. PCA-regression projects onto the top principal components and runs OLS there; partial least squares (PLS) builds components that also correlate with the targets. Both throw away directions before fitting — and a low-variance feature direction can still be highly predictive of a voxel, so hard truncation can discard exactly the signal you wanted. Ridge instead keeps every direction but shrinks each in proportion to how poorly the data constrain it: in the eigenbasis of the feature covariance, a component with eigenvalue d² is multiplied by d²/(d²+λ) — well-measured directions pass nearly untouched, noisy ones are damped smoothly toward zero (Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, ESL §3.4). That graceful, continuous shrinkage — no hard cutoff — is why ridge is the workhorse readout for neural encoding.

How λ is chosen, without cheating

The penalty λ is a hyperparameter, so it must be set on data the final score never sees. The honest protocol is nested cross-validation: an outer split holds out the test set; inside the training portion, an inner cross-validation sweeps a grid of λ values and picks the one with the best inner-held-out error. Only then is the model refit on all of training and scored once on the outer test set. For ridge specifically there is a shortcut — generalized cross-validation (GCV), a closed-form approximation to leave-one-out error (Golub, Heath & Wahba 1979) — that lets you select λ in essentially one fit per target, which is why ridge scales to thousands of voxels at once.

Quick check — why can't you just pick the λ that gives the best test correlation?
Because then the test set has been used to tune the model, and test_corr is no longer an estimate of generalization — it is optimistically biased. λ is a knob; tuning any knob on the test set leaks it into the model. It must be chosen by an inner CV on the training data (or GCV), leaving the test split untouched until the single final scoring.

Banded (fractional) ridge: one λ is not enough

A single λ assumes every feature deserves the same amount of shrinkage. That breaks the moment you do the thing modern encoding models almost always do: concatenate several feature spaces — say low-level Gabor energy and high-level semantic embeddings, or features from two network layers. These bands differ in dimensionality, scale, and how much of the voxel they actually explain. Force one global λ and the larger or noisier band dominates the penalty, and a useful band gets crushed or a useless one runs wild.

Banded ridge regression fixes this by giving each feature group its own λ, fit jointly (Nunez-Elizalde, Huth & Gallant 2019). Per voxel, the optimizer learns how much to trust each space — effectively reweighting the feature spaces by their predictive worth — which both improves prediction and turns the fitted λ's into an interpretable variance-partitioning of the voxel across feature spaces. The remaining obstacle is cost: a separate grid search over many bands is combinatorially expensive. Fractional ridge regression (Rokem & Kay 2020) and the efficient banded solvers of la Tour, Eickenberg, Nunez-Elizalde & Gallant (2022) reparameterize the search so it is tractable at brain scale.

⤇ Going deeper

Banded ridge is the gateway to variance partitioning: re-running with each band alone, then together, lets you ask how much each feature space uniquely explains a region beyond what the others already do — the standard way to adjudicate "is this voxel better explained by low-level or semantic structure?" It is also why feature-space combination is not free: naive concatenation under one λ can score worse than either band alone. If you ever stack feature spaces in a model, this is the failure mode to remember.

06 — The discipline of honesty

Cross-validation & held-out generalization

Everything above hinges on one rule that is easy to state and easy to violate: you must score the model on data it was never fit on. Split the recordings into a train set (used to fit the ridge weights and pick λ) and a test set (touched only once, to score). The model's reward is its performance on the test set alone.

Why so strict? Because overfitting is seductive. Give a flexible model enough freedom and it will memorize the training set — including its noise — achieving near-perfect training correlation while learning nothing that transfers. Training error keeps dropping as complexity grows; test error follows it down at first, then turns and climbs as the model starts fitting noise. The gap between the two curves is the signature of overfitting.

Why how you split matters

A disjoint split is necessary but not sufficient — the split must also prevent leakage, where information about the test set sneaks into training. The right split depends on the data's structure:

  • Images (NSD). The test set is the shared-1000 — 1,000 images shown to every subject — and the readout is fit on the other ~9,000 unique images. The split is image-disjoint: no test image (nor a near-duplicate of it) appears in training, so the model can't win by recognizing a specific picture.
  • Time-series (fMRI, MEG, ECoG). Adjacent samples in time are correlated — a slow fMRI response bleeds across neighbors. A random split would leak: a training sample one TR away from a test sample is almost the same data point. The fix is a contiguous temporal tail — hold out the last stretch by onset time, so the test block is genuinely separated from training.
⚠ Watch out

Random shuffling on time-series is a classic, invisible bug. It inflates the score with no warning because nothing visibly breaks — the numbers just look great and mean nothing. For temporally autocorrelated signals, always split by contiguous blocks of time, never i.i.d. at random.

07 — One number

The metric: Pearson r, per target

How do we turn "predicted vs. recorded" into a score? For each target — each voxel, electrode, or neuron — we compute the Pearson correlation r between the predicted response and the actual response across all test stimuli, then average r over targets. That mean is the reward: test_corr.

Pearson r runs from −1 to +1 and measures how tightly prediction and reality move together, ignoring overall scale and offset (it cares about pattern, not units). An r of 1 is a perfect straight-line relationship; 0 is none. Because brain data is noisy and high-dimensional, the values that matter here are modest — and it helps to calibrate your eye to what a given r actually looks like.

train_corr vs. test_corr: the overfitting tell

The harness reports both train_corr and test_corr. Read them together. A high train_corr paired with a low test_corr is the classic fingerprint of an overfit readout: the ridge has memorized the training set without capturing anything that generalizes. The gap between the two is a health check on the fit — not on the science. The number that ever counts as a claim about the brain is test_corr.

⬢ In this project

test_corr — the mean per-target held-out Pearson r — is the reward, the single scalar the autoresearch loop hill-climbs. For NSD it narrows to one chosen region (NEURO_ROI), though every region's predictivity is logged as corr_<region>. Differences smaller than about 0.005 are noise, not progress.

08 — The ceiling on truth

The noise ceiling & explainable variance

Here is a humbling fact: even a perfect model cannot reach r = 1. Neural responses are noisy — record the same stimulus twice and the responses differ. No model can predict the part of a response that the brain itself doesn't repeat. That irreducible randomness places a hard ceiling on achievable correlation.

We estimate that ceiling from the data's own reliability. Show a stimulus several times and measure how well one repeat predicts another — the split-half or oracle correlation. That reliability is the best any model could possibly do; the variance below it is explainable, the variance above it is noise that no model can or should fit. A model that reaches the ceiling has captured everything the data permits — it is "as good as the data allows," and chasing further is chasing noise. Comparing raw r across regions can mislead, because a region with a low ceiling will look "worse" even when its model is excellent; dividing by the ceiling gives a fairer normalized predictivity.

● In the brain

Ceilings vary widely. Early sensory areas with crisp, repeatable responses (and high-SNR methods like macaque spiking) carry high ceilings; association cortex and noisier methods carry lower ones. So a raw r of 0.25 can represent a better model than an r of 0.40 elsewhere, once each is read against its own ceiling. Always ask: how high could the model have scored here?

Normalizing predictivity: r, R², and what you divide by

"Divide by the ceiling" hides two choices that matter. The first is the metric. Pearson r is a correlation; squared, R² is the fraction of variance explained. They are not interchangeable: an r of 0.4 explains only 16% of the variance, and the gap widens as r shrinks — so a chart in R² looks far more sobering than the same chart in r. Normalized scores are usually reported as variance ratios: the model's explained variance over the explainable (ceiling) variance, sometimes written as a normalized R² or, after taking the square root, a noise-ceiling-normalized correlation.

The second choice is what the targets are. If you predict the average of several repeats of each stimulus, the target is far cleaner and the achievable correlation rises toward a ceiling set by how many repeats you averaged. If you predict single-trial responses, the ceiling is much lower — trial noise is in every target — and a model that looks mediocre on single trials can be excellent once you normalize. Because the ceiling depends on repeat count and SNR, an estimator that corrects for it is essential: Spearman's correction for attenuation, and the signal-power / normalized correlation estimators (CCnorm) of Schoppe, Harper, Willmore, King & Schnupp (2016), give an unbiased denominator. The single most common error here is comparing raw r across regions or methods with different ceilings — an apples-to-oranges comparison that the normalization exists to prevent.

Quick check — region X scores raw r = 0.30 (ceiling 0.45); region Y scores raw r = 0.42 (ceiling 0.85). Which model is doing better?
Region X. Normalized, X reaches 0.30/0.45 ≈ 0.67 of its ceiling while Y reaches 0.42/0.85 ≈ 0.49. The higher raw number (Y) is the worse model relative to what was achievable there — exactly the trap that ceiling-normalization is built to catch.
08b — Three lenses on one question

Encoding, decoding & RSA — and how predictivity adjudicates

Encoding is one of three standard ways to relate a representation to brain activity. All three start from the same raw material — a set of stimuli and the brain responses they evoke — but they ask different questions, and each can conclude different things. Knowing which lens answers which question keeps you from over-claiming.

Encoding fits a forward map from model features to each neural target and scores held-out prediction; a high score says the features are sufficient, and linearly accessible, to account for the responses. Decoding fits the reverse map and asks whether the stimulus (or a label) can be recovered from activity; success says the information is present and extractable, but not how it is represented. Representational similarity analysis (RSA) skips fitting a map entirely: it compares the geometry of the two spaces — the matrix of pairwise dissimilarities between stimuli in the model vs. in the brain — and asks whether stimuli that are far apart in the model are far apart in cortex (Kriegeskorte, Mur & Bandettini 2008). RSA needs no shared feature dimensions and so compares spaces of different formats, but it is correlational about structure, not predictive about responses.

Using predictivity to compare hypotheses

Where encoding earns its keep is model comparison. Because every candidate is scored by the same held-out predictivity against the same targets, two representational hypotheses can be put on one axis and the data made to choose between them: the feature space that predicts a region better is, on this evidence, the better account of what that region computes. This is precisely how the field reads results — ranking network layers against cortical hierarchy, or asking whether a region is better explained by low-level or semantic features (variance partitioning, from the banded-ridge section). Two cautions keep it honest. First, normalize before you rank: compare against the noise ceiling, since a model can only ever chase the explainable variance. Second, beware confounds — two feature spaces that are themselves correlated will post similar scores, so a difference is only evidence if the spaces are genuinely distinct. Kriegeskorte & Diedrichsen (2019) and Naselaris et al. (2011) lay out this comparison logic in full.

⤇ Going deeper

The three lenses are not rivals but a triangulation. RSA's strength — comparing spaces with no common dimensions — is also its limit: it tests second-order structure (distances), so two representations with the same geometry but different content can look identical. Encoding's voxel-wise fit is more demanding and more diagnostic, but needs the feature and brain spaces in a fittable relation. Decoding bounds what information is there. A strong claim — "this region computes X" — is best supported when an encoding model wins on predictivity, an RSA confirms the geometry, and the result survives the noise-ceiling normalization.

09 — What prediction does and doesn't buy

Prediction ≈ understanding (with one honest caveat)

Put the pieces together and you have a complete scientific instrument. A feature hypothesis is made explicit as a fixed model; a linear readout tests what it makes accessible; ridge keeps that readout honest; a disjoint split keeps the score honest; Pearson r measures it; and the noise ceiling tells you how good "good" could ever be. A model that predicts held-out responses near the ceiling has, in the strongest operational sense available, captured the computation the region performs.

And yet — a caveat worth holding onto. Prediction is not proof of identical mechanism. Two different systems can produce the same input–output mapping; matching a brain region's responses shows your features are sufficient to explain them, not that the brain implements those features the same way. A high test_corr is powerful evidence and a real constraint on theories, but it is a behavioral-level claim about representation, not a wiring diagram. Chapter 06 returns to exactly this tension — how far the striking match between deep networks and the brain can be pushed, and where it breaks.

That is the empirical engine of this project. The bar to beat is concrete: a trained ImageNet AlexNet (fc7, 4096-d), read out with the same ridge. Per region (Pearson r): V1 0.342 · V2 0.299 · V3 0.321 · hV4 0.289 · FFA 0.396 · PPA 0.403 · EBA 0.436 · VWFA 0.321 · ventral 0.284 · overall 0.324. Empirically, hand-designed untrained models match or beat it in early/mid visual cortex (V1–V3) but fall short in high-level regions (FFA/PPA/EBA/VWFA) — a clean probe of what architecture gives you for free versus what learning has to buy.

Pick an answer to see why it's right or wrong. Score: 0 / 8

  1. 1. A lab reports that, from fMRI activity alone, they can tell whether a subject was viewing a face or a house with 92% accuracy. What kind of model is this, and what does it license them to conclude?

    Why: Reading the stimulus from activity is decoding. Decoding establishes that the relevant information is present and recoverable, but a decoder can succeed off an incidental statistical crumb (e.g. a global activity difference) without testing any hypothesis about how faces are represented. Sufficiency/linear-accessibility claims belong to encoding.

  2. 2. Why is held-out (cross-validated) prediction, rather than goodness-of-fit on the training data, taken as the test of whether a model "understands" a brain region?

    Why: Goodness-of-fit on training data can be driven to near-perfection by memorizing noise, so it proves nothing about the underlying computation. Only prediction on stimuli the model was never fit on converts a claim into a falsifiable wager. (Option D is a red herring — training fit is still computable when p>n, it just becomes meaninglessly perfect.)

  3. 3. Ridge regression deliberately shrinks coefficients toward zero. In bias–variance terms, why does this usually improve held-out prediction with many collinear features?

    Why: Shrinkage is a bias–variance trade: a little bias is added, but the variance of the estimate — which explodes under collinearity and p≈n — drops sharply, and on noisy held-out data the lower-variance solution wins. Ridge's training error is actually higher than OLS (option C is false); the point is test error, not train error.

  4. 4. On a time-series dataset (fMRI/MEG/ECoG), a model reports train_corr = 0.71 and test_corr = 0.68 using a random train/test split. What is the most likely problem?

    Why: Adjacent time samples are correlated, so a random split places near-duplicate samples on both sides — classic leakage. Nothing visibly breaks; the score just looks great and means little. The fix is a contiguous temporal tail. A suspiciously high, train-like test score on shuffled time-series is the tell.

  5. 5. A region has a noise ceiling of 0.45. Model A reaches raw r = 0.30 there; in a different region (ceiling 0.85) Model B reaches raw r = 0.42. Which is the stronger model, and why?

    Why: Raw r is not comparable across regions with different ceilings. Normalized by what was achievable, Model A captures ~67% of its explainable variance vs. Model B's ~49%, so A is the better model despite the lower raw number. This is exactly why predictivity is normalized before models are ranked.

  6. 6. Why is the stage-2 readout kept linear and regularized instead of a powerful deep decoder, even though the deep decoder would score higher?

    Why: Linearity pins the credit on the representation: a linear map can only reweight and sum what the features already make explicit. A deep decoder could rescue a weak feature space by doing the untangling in stage two, making the score a verdict on the decoder, not the hypothesis. (Linear readouts can absolutely overfit — that is why they are regularized; option D is false.)

  7. 7. You concatenate a wide low-level (Gabor) feature space with a narrow semantic embedding and fit them under a single ridge λ. Banded ridge would likely help most because:

    Why: One global λ assumes both spaces deserve equal shrinkage; in practice the wide/noisy band can crush a narrow useful one. Banded ridge gives each band its own λ, fit jointly per voxel, which improves prediction and yields an interpretable variance split (Nunez-Elizalde et al. 2019). It still needs CV and is not per-band OLS.

  8. 8. A feature model predicts a region's held-out responses right up to its noise ceiling. What is the strongest claim this licenses?

    Why: Prediction establishes sufficiency, not identity: distinct systems can share an input–output mapping, so matching responses shows your features can explain them, not that the brain wires them the same way. It is also not a uniqueness claim — another feature space could match it — and the ceiling reflects irreducible noise, which never vanishes.

Key terms

The core ideas this chapter rests on. Every later chapter assumes them.

Encoding model
A model that predicts brain activity from a stimulus (or features of it). The generative test of a representational hypothesis — the opposite direction from a decoder, and the kind neuro-autoresearch builds.
Linear readout
A weighted-sum mapping from feature activations to predicted neural response. Kept linear on purpose, so a high score credits the features (what they make explicit), not a clever decoder.
Ridge regression
Least-squares fitting with an L2 penalty that shrinks coefficients toward zero. The harness's stage-2 readout; stable where ordinary least squares fails (many collinear features, p ≈ n).
Regularization
Adding a penalty (here L2, strength λ) to a fit to trade a little bias for much less variance, improving generalization. The bias–variance trade-off in action.
Cross-validation
Scoring a model on a held-out split it was never fit on, with λ chosen on training data. The split must be disjoint and leakage-free (image-disjoint for NSD; contiguous temporal tail for time-series).
Pearson r
Correlation between predicted and recorded responses per target, averaged across targets = test_corr, the reward. Scale-free, from −1 to +1; project-level rewards live around 0.3–0.4.
Noise ceiling
The maximum correlation any model could reach, set by the data's own trial-to-trial reliability (split-half / oracle). Variance below it is explainable; above it is unfittable noise. Enables normalized predictivity — and the ceiling itself is lower for single-trial targets than for averaged repeats.
Banded ridge
Ridge with a separate penalty λ per feature group, fit jointly (Nunez-Elizalde et al. 2019). Essential when combining feature spaces: it stops one band from dominating the penalty and yields a per-voxel variance split across spaces.
RSA
Representational similarity analysis — compares the geometry (pairwise dissimilarity matrices) of a model and the brain without fitting a map. The third lens alongside encoding and decoding; tests structural correspondence, not response prediction.
Overfitting
Fitting the training set's noise instead of its signal: high train_corr, low test_corr. The gap between the two is the tell; only the test score is a claim about the brain.
