How to read this

The chapters build on each other — Chapter 1 sets up the central idea (an encoding model: predict brain activity from a stimulus), and Chapter 6 closes the loop back to the project's thesis. But each stands alone, so dip in wherever your curiosity points.

Throughout, an “In this project” note connects each concept to what neuro·autoresearch actually does.

Every chapter has quick-check prompts as you read and an interactive quiz at the end — and there's a cross-chapter final exam below once you've been through them.

The final exam

Do the ideas connect?

Ten questions pulled across all six chapters — pick an answer to see why it's right or wrong. Score: 0 / 10

  1. 1. A proposed model scores train_corr 0.61 but test_corr 0.10. The most likely diagnosis?

    Why: High train + low test is the signature of an overfit readout. Only generalization to held-out data (test_corr) counts as evidence the model captured the computation. (Ch. 1)

  2. 2. Why read out a fixed model with a linear ridge map instead of a deep decoder?

    Why: The linear-readout hypothesis: information is "usable" if a downstream neuron could extract it with a weighted sum. A powerful decoder would conflate "the features contain it" with "the features make it explicit." (Ch. 1 & 4)

  3. 3. Which method offers both millimetre spatial and millisecond temporal resolution — but only in clinical patients?

    Why: Going inside the skull buys both axes at once, but requires electrodes implanted for clinical (e.g. epilepsy) reasons — the trade-off is invasiveness. (Ch. 3)

  4. 4. MEG is relatively blind to which neural sources?

    Why: A purely radial current dipole produces almost no field outside a spherical conductor, so MEG mainly senses tangential (sulcal) sources — complementary to EEG. (Ch. 3)

  5. 5. Nonlinear "mixed selectivity" in a population is useful because it…

    Why: High-dimensional mixed codes let a simple linear readout carve out many more category boundaries — capacity, not economy, is the point. (Ch. 2)

  6. 6. "Untangling" along the ventral stream means…

    Why: In pixel space, images of one object under different views form a tangled manifold; the hierarchy progressively warps representations so a linear readout can separate identity. (Ch. 4)

  7. 7. Untrained / random CNNs predict V1–V3 well but fall short in FFA/PPA. Best explanation?

    Why: This is the project's central probe of architecture vs learning: wiring + natural-image statistics get you early vision; semantics seem to need optimization. (Ch. 4 & 6)

  8. 8. The N400 ERP component primarily indexes…

    Why: The N400 grows for less-expected words (higher surprisal); the P600 is the one tied to syntactic reanalysis. (Ch. 5)

  9. 9. Why do GPT-style contextual embeddings linearly predict language-cortex responses to the same words?

    Why: Convergent objectives produce convergent representations; the LM→brain fit scaling with model quality is a striking encoding-model result. (Ch. 5 & 6)

  10. 10. A model predicts held-out responses right up to the noise ceiling. Does this prove the brain implements that mechanism?

    Why: Strong prediction is necessary but not sufficient. Different architectures often reach indistinguishable scores, so a good fit constrains — but doesn't pin down — the underlying computation. (Ch. 1 & 6)
