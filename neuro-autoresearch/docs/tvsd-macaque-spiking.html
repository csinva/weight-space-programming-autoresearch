Field note 00 — Orientation

When a monkey looks at a photograph of a cup, electricity ripples through a chain of cortical areas — V1 V4 IT — and somewhere in that cascade the abstract idea "cup" becomes legible. The THINGS Ventral-stream Spiking Dataset (TVSD) is a rare, direct window onto that cascade: not a blood-flow shadow, but the firing of neurons themselves.

This page is a field-guide to three things. First, the primate ventral visual stream, the brain's core object-recognition pathway. Second, the signal — what spikes and multi-unit activity recorded from Utah microelectrode arrays actually are. And third, how our neuro-autoresearch harness turns this dataset into a benchmark for fixed, never-trained encoding models.

Key terms

Ventral stream
The "what" pathway — the occipito-temporal sequence V1 → V2 → V4 → IT that builds invariant object identity.
Spike
A neuron's action potential: the ~1 ms all-or-nothing voltage event that is the cortex's unit of communication.
MUA
Multi-unit activity — the pooled spiking of the small population of neurons near one electrode tip.
Encoding model
A function from stimulus → predicted neural response. The better it predicts held-out responses, the more brain-like it is.

Field note 01 — Anatomy

The primate ventral “what” stream

Vision is not a snapshot. It is a hierarchy of transformations that turns a grid of retinal light into the knowledge that you are looking at a particular object.

Light from the eye reaches primary visual cortex — V1 — at the back of the brain, and from there flows forward and downward along the temporal lobe. Each step abstracts a little more. The receptive field (the patch of the visual world a neuron "watches") grows larger; the features a neuron prefers grow more complex; and, crucially, the representation grows more invariant — a face stays a face whether it is near or far, left or right, large or small.

The climb, one stage at a time

  • V1 — oriented edges. Neurons act like small, local edge-and-bar detectors at specific orientations and spatial frequencies. Receptive fields are tiny.
  • V4 — contours, texture, color. A mid-level station tuned to curvature, surface texture and hue. Receptive fields are larger; parts begin to combine into shapes.
  • IT — objects & identity. Inferotemporal cortex hosts neurons selective for complex objects and faces, and — the headline property — for identity that holds across changes in position, size, and pose.

Untangling identity: IT makes the "cup" of a cup explicit in a way the raw pixels never were.

Researchers describe IT as producing an untangled representation — one where simple linear readouts can separate object categories that were hopelessly interleaved at the pixel and V1 level. That is precisely the property an encoding-model benchmark can probe: how much of each area's response can a fixed model explain?

Field note 02 — Instrumentation

The signal: spikes, MUA & Utah arrays

A neuron speaks in spikes — brief, stereotyped voltage pulses. An electrode in the cortex overhears them. The trick is turning that overheard chatter into one clean number per image.

When a neuron fires, it produces an action potential (a "spike"): an all-or-nothing electrical event lasting about a millisecond. The pattern and rate of spikes is the cortex's code. A microelectrode placed near a neuron picks up the tiny extracellular signature of that spike.

If a recording is clean enough to attribute every spike to one identified neuron, you have single-unit activity. More often, an electrode hears a handful of nearby cells at once — that pooled signal is multi-unit activity (MUA). MUA is robust and stable over long chronic recordings, which is exactly what you want when you intend to show a monkey tens of thousands of images.

TVSD ships normalized MUA: for each electrode, the windowed envelope of multi-unit spiking evoked by an image is collapsed to a single value — one number per electrode, per image. That table, [electrodes × images], is the target our harness predicts.

Single vs. multi unit

Single-unit (SUA)
Spikes sorted to one isolated neuron. High specificity, harder to maintain over months.
Multi-unit (MUA)
The pooled spiking of the local neuron cluster near the electrode tip — what TVSD records.
Normalized MUA
The MUA response envelope in a fixed time window, baseline-corrected and scaled, reduced to one value per image.
Chronic array
An electrode grid implanted to stay in place, enabling stable recordings across many sessions.

Field note 03 — The recording

The experiment

Two macaques watched a flood of natural images while every implanted electrode logged its evoked firing. The result is one of the largest ventral-stream spiking datasets ever released.

Where the electrodes sit

Each monkey's 1,024 electrodes are distributed across the three sampled areas — V1, V4, IT — but the split differs by animal:

  • Monkey F — V1 512 · IT 320 · V4 192
  • Monkey N — V1 512 · V4 256 · IT 256

Both animals devote half their channels to V1, but they trade off the remainder between V4 and IT — so each monkey gives a slightly different vantage on the high-level end of the stream.

The stimuli & the split

The images come from the THINGS database (Hebart et al.) — a large, systematically sampled collection of naturalistic photographs of everyday objects, built to span the breadth of nameable things. Each image is shown and the evoked response logged; the dataset then arrives pre-averaged as the [electrodes × images] table, with its own fixed split: 22,248 training images seen once each, and 100 test images repeated many times so their responses can be averaged into a stable estimate. The split is image-disjoint by construction — no test picture appears in training.

Noise ceiling — why high scores are reachable

Because the 100 test images are repeated, the release can ship an oracle split-half correlation per electrode: split the repeats in two, correlate the two halves, and you get the ceiling on how predictable that electrode could be given its own trial-to-trial noise. Across electrodes this oracle averages roughly 0.55–0.63 — so an excellent encoding model has real headroom, and a high test_corr is genuinely attainable rather than capped near zero by noise.

The data come from Papale et al., Neuron (2025), out of the Roelfsema lab at the Netherlands Institute for Neuroscience. The THINGS images themselves are gated (a license plus roughly 12 GB of pixels) and are not bundled with the spiking table — they are staged separately before any run.

Field note 04 — Brains & machines

Why this matters — for brains and AI

The ventral stream and the convolutional neural network grew up entangled. Measuring real cortex against models closes the loop.

The architecture that defines modern computer vision — stacked layers of local filters, pooling, and increasing abstraction — was directly inspired by the visual cortex's hierarchy. The intellectual lineage runs from Hubel & Wiesel's edge detectors in V1, through Fukushima's Neocognitron, to today's deep convolutional networks.

The loop then closed the other way. Work from Yamins, DiCarlo and colleagues (2014, 2016) showed that goal-driven deep networks — trained to recognize objects — became the best available predictive models of IT: the more a network's task performance improved, the better its internal activations predicted real IT neurons. That insight powers the community Brain-Score effort, which ranks models by how well they match primate ventral-stream data.

A model that predicts a neuron is, in a real sense, a theory of what that neuron computes.

TVSD lets you put any candidate model — even an untrained one — directly up against real primate cortical firing. That is rare and valuable: unlike fMRI or MEG, which measure blood flow or scalp magnetic fields, this is the firing of cortex itself. The benchmark question becomes sharp and honest: how much of V1, V4, or IT can your fixed model explain?

Field note 05 — In this codebase

How neuro-autoresearch uses it

The harness frames every dataset the same way: an encoding model. A fixed, never-trained model turns the stimulus into activations; a ridge regression reads those out to predict recorded neural activity; held-out correlation is the reward.

The concrete contract

  • Stimulus X. The shown THINGS image, resized to 16×16 RGB and flattened to [0,1] → 16·16·3 = 768 dims (RawPixels).
  • Target Y. Each electrode's normalized MUA for the chosen area — one value per electrode per image.
  • Reward area. One of V1 V4 IT via NEURO_ROI (default IT); the ridge fits only that area's electrodes.
  • Monkey. F or N via NEURO_SUBJECT; each (monkey, area) writes its own CSV.
  • Score. test_corr = mean per-electrode Pearson r over the 100 held-out test images.

Near-perfect ground truth: this is the firing of cortex itself, not a blood-flow shadow.

Because the target is spiking activity rather than an indirect, sluggish proxy like fMRI's BOLD signal, an encoding model here is judged against an unusually direct measurement of what neurons actually did. The noise ceiling above (~0.55–0.63) says how good a model could ever be — and how much room there still is to climb.

Run it

# reward = IT in monkey F, scored over the 100 held-out THINGS test images
NEURO_DATASET=tvsd NEURO_ROI=IT NEURO_SUBJECT=F python -m harness.run

# swap the reward area / monkey freely
NEURO_DATASET=tvsd NEURO_ROI=V1 NEURO_SUBJECT=N python -m harness.run

The search uses monkey F. The evaluation experiment scores the unchanged champion and the same pretrained and untrained vision baselines separately on monkey F and held-out monkey N, retaining the individual-animal results rather than treating their difference as a model gap.
